*As artificial intelligence enters race strategy, engineering and team operations, the contest for milliseconds is expanding beyond the car itself.*

## 1. The Search for Milliseconds

Formula 1 has always been decided by small margins, and the 2025 season showed how small they can be. Lando Norris entered the Abu Dhabi finale 12 points clear of Max Verstappen and left it with the drivers’ title by just two, 423 points to 421 ([PlanetF1](https://www.planetf1.com/news/final-f1-points-standings-after-abu-dhabi-grand-prix)). Over 24 races, a gap of that size can come down to a single mistimed pit stop, one penalty avoided or incurred, or a tyre choice that proved a few laps too optimistic.

Teams have always chased such margins through aerodynamics, power units and pit stop practice. What has changed in 2026 is where a growing share of the effort goes. Increasingly it is directed at software that helps people decide, rather than hardware that helps a car go faster. Reuters, citing Ampere Analysis, reported that eight new AI partnerships were signed across Formula 1 and its 11 teams in six months. Technology led team spending categories at an estimated $769 million last season, up 41 per cent, and AI and machine learning brands accounted for four of the top 15 new sponsorship investors ([Reuters via Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/ai-partnerships-surge-across-formula-one-teams-reshape-racing-strategy-126050401844_1.html)).

Reading this as a marketing trend alone would be a mistake. The traditional sponsorship template put a logo on a sidepod. The newer one puts the sponsor’s product inside the team’s working day. Peter Kenyon, a board adviser at Williams, told Reuters the Anthropic deal is “much more than a sticker on a car” and that the team sees it as a potential differentiator.

The question for the next several seasons is therefore not whether teams use AI, since nearly all of them now do. It is which teams convert it into lap time and better decisions, and why. This article looks at where AI is being applied, what evidence exists for its value, where its limits lie, and what may separate the teams that benefit from those that mainly carry the branding.

## 2. A Sport Built on Data

Formula 1 cars are among the most heavily instrumented machines in sport. When Microsoft announced its multiyear partnership with Mercedes in January, coverage of the deal put the data stream from each car at roughly 1.1 million data points per second ([Mobile World Live](https://www.mobileworldlive.com/microsoft/microsoft-scores-f1-deal-with-mercedes/)). Add simulation output, tyre models, weather feeds, radio, regulatory documents and factory records, and the total exceeds what any group of engineers can read unaided.

Large data volumes are not new to the sport. What is new is the ability to query that information in plain language, at speed, and to have software propose an action. Three developments seem to me the most significant.

First, cloud and AI platforms are now part of race infrastructure. Mercedes’ agreement with Microsoft covers cloud and enterprise AI from the factory to the circuit ([Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/01/22/microsoft-and-mercedes-amg-petronas-f1-team-unite-to-drive-innovation-from-factory-to-circuit/)). McLaren has said it runs close to 300 million race simulations before a weekend, a figure that comes from a 2024 Google Cloud case study ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/transform/mclaren-racing-miami-grand-prix-win-ai-cloud-performance-innovation)).

Second, language models are being applied to the regulatory record. McLaren and Google Cloud built a Gemini-powered tool that lets trackside engineers search and compare dense sporting regulations during a race ([McLaren](https://www.mclaren.com/racing/formula-1/2026/british-grand-prix/heritage-inspired-google-gemini-livery/)). Red Bull says its own system began as a search tool for regulations and past rulings ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/red-bull-explains-how-ai-is-influencing-its-f1-race-strategy/10865844/)). With technical, sporting and financial rules layered on top of years of steward decisions, this is a sensible first use case. The information is text, the questions are repetitive and the cost of a slow answer is high.

Third, the rights holder and the governing body are using the same tools. F1 and AWS built a root cause assistant for race-day operational problems that AWS says cut initial triage from more than a day to under 20 minutes ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-formula-1-uses-generative-ai-to-accelerate-race-day-issue-resolution)). In October the FIA named AWS its official AI and cloud computing partner. It will use AWS AI tools when drafting future technical regulations and already runs aerodynamic simulations on AWS high-performance computing ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/fia-partners-with-aws-to-help-drive-future-f1-regulations/10868912/)). Earlier this year the FIA also agreed a weather partnership with Tomorrow.io, whose AI-driven forecasting feeds race control and bespoke portals for teams ([The Race](https://www.the-race.com/formula-1/f1-weather-revolution-forecasting-ai/)).

The editorial point is that the data and the tooling are now widely available. Every serious team can buy access to capable models and cloud capacity. What differs is how well each organisation collects, labels and trusts its own information. A strong model cannot compensate for fragmented systems, and Williams has given a candid illustration of that, which I return to below.

## 3. Machine Learning and Race Strategy

Race strategy is a decision problem under uncertainty. When to stop, which compound to fit, whether to react to a safety car, how to answer a rival who has just pitted: each choice depends on tyre degradation, track position, weather and the expected behaviour of other teams. The conventional approach, as researchers at King’s College London describe it, is to build baseline strategies and run them through thousands of simulations. Those simulations are slow and tend to underplay how teams react to one another during a race ([King’s College London](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/ai-driven-race-strategy-could-give-formula-one-teams-competitive-advantage)).

A study led from King’s, reported in June, tested an alternative. The team trained a reinforcement learning model, called RSRL, on races across recent seasons and paired it with explainable AI so that strategists could review why it chose what it did. In simulations of Lewis Hamilton’s 2023 Bahrain Grand Prix, the model achieved an average finishing position of 5.33. The best non-AI method managed 5.63, and the car’s expected finish was 5.50. The model also reproduced an undercut, stopping earlier than a rival to benefit from fresher tyres, without being taught the tactic. A separate paper from ETH Zurich researchers, posted in February, takes a similar learning-based approach to multi-agent race strategy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23056)).

I would read these results with care but not dismiss them. A third of a position in one simulated race is a small effect, and a simulation is not a grand prix, since rival cars do not adapt the way real strategists do. Yet small effects compound across a 24-race calendar and across two cars, and the 2025 title was settled by two points. The explainability component matters as much as the accuracy. Strategists will not act on a recommendation they cannot interrogate, and a model that cannot justify itself will be ignored at exactly the moment it is needed.

Industry deployments are further along on workflow than on pure optimisation. Red Bull and Oracle announced in February a strategy agent designed to work alongside engineers, using automated collection and interpretation of past and live race inputs ([Reuters via BusinessDay](https://www.businessday.co.za/sport/f-one/2026-02-26-oracle-and-red-bull-extend-f1-partnership-with-ai-boost/)). Jack Harington of Red Bull says the tool has moved from searching to recommending, and that “it’s actually making decisions for us.”

The most instructive example is Monza in 2025. Verstappen ran off at the first chicane while fighting Norris for the lead. According to Harington, the agent advised handing the position back to avoid a penalty. That ran against the team’s own reading of the situation, but Red Bull followed it, Verstappen was not penalised, retook the lead and won by 19.2 seconds ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/red-bull-explains-how-ai-is-influencing-its-f1-race-strategy/10865844/)).

I find this use case more interesting than lap time prediction. Team principal Laurent Mekies has explained that the agent brings together technical, sporting and financial regulations with the history of live and post-race judgments, so the team can query the likelihood of being punished for a given move. A penalty is a probabilistic cost, and a system that has absorbed years of stewarding decisions may price it more consistently than a person under pressure. The caveat is that this is a single example chosen by the team. Outside observers do not see the occasions on which the agent was wrong or unhelpful, and the sport would benefit from teams sharing something closer to a hit rate.

Oracle’s co-CEO Clay Magouyrk has suggested that an AI strategist could eventually call the race in real time, with a person still making the final decision ([Reuters via BusinessDay](https://www.businessday.co.za/sport/f-one/2026-02-26-oracle-and-red-bull-extend-f1-partnership-with-ai-boost/)). That is plausible. It is also the point at which the governance questions discussed later in this piece begin to matter.

## 4. Beyond the Pit Wall

Much of the value in AI is found away from the pit wall. This mirrors what happens in other industries, and it deserves more attention than the strategy headlines receive.

Williams offers the clearest public account. According to City AM, the team has rolled out Atlassian software across a workforce of about 1,200. During practice at the Hungarian Grand Prix, a system failure prevented the team from collecting data from an important test. It later emerged that the same fault had occurred at the factory two weeks earlier but never reached the trackside staff, because fault reporting was not joined up. Williams has since moved more of its factory and trackside fault reporting into Jira Service Management, where Atlassian’s Rovo assistant can flag likely duplicate problems and retrieve earlier incidents and how they were resolved ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/williams-turns-to-ai-to-squeeze-more-from-f1-cost-cap/)).

Nothing about this is glamorous, and that is the point. A model is useful only if the right information reaches it and the people who need the answer. Matt Harman, Williams’ director of engineering, said the team’s constraint is time rather than money, and that he does not want AI to replace the questioning habits of engineers. He wants it to assemble information and suggest a conclusion that people then test. Atlassian reports that an initial programme with 200 staff removed 863 hours of low-value meetings in a month and raised work throughput by 83 per cent. Those figures are Atlassian’s own assessment, from a relationship in which it has a commercial interest, and should be treated accordingly.

Williams has also made Anthropic’s Claude its Official Thinking Partner under a multi-year agreement, with the model to be used across race strategy, car development and operational decision-making ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/zh-hans/newsevents/news/2026/02/loeb-represents-anthropic-in-multi-year-partnership-with-williams-formula-1-team)). The team stood ninth in the constructors’ standings after the Brazilian Grand Prix, with 12 points ([GPFans](https://www.gpfans.com/en/f1-news/1092671/f1-standings-brazilian-grand-prix-hamilton-loses-ground-verstappen-mercedes-champions/)). That does not show the partnership has failed. The deal is in its first season, the benefits of better information flow build slowly, and car performance remains the dominant variable. It does show that AI sits downstream of the quality of the car and cannot be expected to offset a deficit on its own.

Engineering is the second major area. Neural Concept’s platform, which Williams has acknowledged using, learns from earlier CFD and wind tunnel data to propose aerodynamic shapes. TechCrunch reported in 2024 that because such a system makes learned predictions rather than running physics-based calculations, it is largely outside the restrictions that govern formal testing ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/14/how-neural-concepts-aerodynamic-ai-is-shaping-formula-one)). Racing Bulls announced a partnership with the company ahead of the 2026 regulation change ([Black Book Motorsport](https://www.blackbookmotorsport.com/news/racing-bulls-f1-ai-neural-concept-partnership-june-2025/)). The testing restrictions exist to limit how much a well-funded team can explore. A learned surrogate lets every permitted test run inform many more candidate designs. My expectation is that this gap between the letter and the intent of the testing limits will draw regulatory attention sooner than any on-track use of AI will.

That brings the discussion to money. The cost cap stands at $215 million this year, and Reuters reports that AI is seen as a tool for navigating both the new technical rules and the new cap ([Reuters via Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/ai-partnerships-surge-across-formula-one-teams-reshape-racing-strategy-126050401844_1.html)). Efficiency is valuable under a cap. But a cap designed to limit what money can buy raises a question about in-kind support from technology partners, such as donated compute or embedded engineers, and how it should be valued. I could not find a published FIA position on this point specific to AI partnerships. It is a question the governing body will need to answer before the practice becomes entrenched.

## 5. Where Machines Meet Their Limits

At the Singapore Grand Prix in October, Lewis Hamilton committed to a one-stop strategy that required 44 laps on a single set of tyres. Ferrari had expected him to pit under a virtual safety car. He later apologised to the team, and Martin Brundle observed that managing strategy from the cockpit of a modern car is difficult ([PlanetF1](https://www.planetf1.com/news/lewis-hamilton-ferrari-strategy-f1-2026-singapore-grand-prix-martin-brundle)).

Set that beside Monza. In one case a team followed a machine recommendation against its own judgement, with a good result. In the other a driver overrode his team’s plan, with a poor one. Neither episode supports a general rule. Deferring to the model is not safe by default, and neither is overriding it. What matters is calibration: knowing when a situation resembles the data a system was built on, and when it does not.

That second condition is a real vulnerability in 2026. Models trained on historical races learn from earlier regulations. This year’s rules added energy management and new boost and overtake modes, which Mekies said added dimensions to an already complex problem ([Reuters via BusinessDay](https://www.businessday.co.za/sport/f-one/2026-02-26-oracle-and-red-bull-extend-f1-partnership-with-ai-boost/)). McLaren’s Mark Temple has said the tactics of racing in 2026 are quite different from before ([McLaren](https://www.mclaren.com/racing/formula-1/2026/how-racing-has-changed-since-the-2026-rule-change/)). A strategy model trained on the previous era has less relevant history to draw on, and the teams that retrain and validate fastest should benefit most. Rare events compound the problem. Safety cars, first-lap incidents and sudden weather changes are the cases where historical data is thinnest and the stakes are highest. The co-founder of Tomorrow.io was candid that a forecast is a model rather than the real thing, and that predicting weather calls for humility ([The Race](https://www.the-race.com/formula-1/f1-weather-revolution-forecasting-ai/)). The same applies to strategy.

The second limit is evidence. Public information about AI performance comes mostly from the teams and vendors that benefit from favourable coverage. Atlassian’s measurements are its own, the AWS results concern F1’s operations rather than team performance, and Red Bull chose the Monza example. None of this suggests bad faith. It means the sport lacks independent measures of whether AI improves decisions.

The third limit is the scoreboard. Mercedes won both championships in 2026 and took the first six races of the season ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/sports/f1/mercedes-complete-formula-one-title-double-in-brazil?amp=1)), with Kimi Antonelli becoming the youngest world champion in the sport’s history ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/mercedes-crowned-2026-f1-constructors-champion-at-brazilian-gp/10873045/)). Red Bull, whose agentic system has had the most public attention, finished third in the constructors’ standings behind Mercedes and Ferrari ([GPFans](https://www.gpfans.com/en/f1-news/1092671/f1-standings-brazilian-grand-prix-hamilton-loses-ground-verstappen-mercedes-champions/)). That was also its first year with its own power unit, which Mekies said Oracle’s cloud and AI helped develop, so the picture is not one of AI failing. It is one of AI being a secondary factor. The order in 2026 was shaped chiefly by the new regulations: the field started the year at its widest spread since 2017 ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/weds-f1s-2026-reset-spreads-field-out-to-its-widest-since-2017/10812731/)) and later tightened considerably. On public evidence, AI has not yet been the deciding factor in any team’s season, and I would be sceptical of anyone who claims otherwise.

## 6. The Next Competitive Frontier

My view is that AI capability will become a genuine differentiator between teams, though not in the way the partnership announcements imply.

First, access will not be what separates teams. Williams has Anthropic, Red Bull has Oracle, McLaren has Google, Mercedes has Microsoft and Aston Martin has CoreWeave, while Ferrari works with IBM on fan-facing experiences ([Reuters via Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/ai-partnerships-surge-across-formula-one-teams-reshape-racing-strategy-126050401844_1.html); [Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/red-bull-explains-how-ai-is-influencing-its-f1-race-strategy/10865844/)). When every team has capable models, advantage moves to what surrounds them: proprietary data, how tightly the tools sit inside decision workflows, how well simulation correlates with the track, and engineers with the skill and independence to challenge outputs. That is organisational capability, and organisational capability has always separated Formula 1 teams.

Second, the development race is where I would look first. This season showed how fast the order can change. A team that finds, validates and ships upgrades more quickly keeps an advantage for longer, and AI applied to aerodynamic exploration and to the flow of engineering information shortens that loop. A team that learns faster compounds the gain across a season.

Third, the cost cap changes who benefits. Under a cap, the measure that matters is decision quality per dollar. In principle AI can help smaller or recovering teams do more with a fixed budget, and Williams says it sees AI as a way to pursue more projects rather than cut staff. In practice the benefit will depend on whether a team can attract partners willing to supply engineering time as well as software, and the most visible teams are likely to be the most attractive to partners.

Three developments will show whether this becomes a lasting lever or a fashionable line item.

**Regulation.** The open questions are how testing restrictions apply to learned surrogate models, how in-kind AI support is treated under the cost cap, and whether any rules will govern automated recommendations during a race. The FIA’s partnership with AWS gives it welcome analytical capacity. It also means that the governing body, the commercial rights holder and several teams rely on overlapping suppliers, given that AWS has been F1’s innovation technology partner since 2018 ([Motorsport.com](https://au.motorsport.com/f1/news/fia-partners-with-aws-to-help-drive-future-f1-regulations/10868912/)). That is not improper, but it makes transparency about how the FIA uses such tools more important.

**Evidence.** Teams and vendors should be asked for measures that matter: strategy errors avoided, penalties avoided and upgrade cycle times, rather than hours saved in meetings. Independent analysis of decision quality, even with a lag, would do more for the credibility of these partnerships than another announcement.

**Visibility.** As recommendations from software begin to shape outcomes on track, fans, broadcasters and stewards will ask who made a call and on what basis. Teams will guard those details, as they guard most things. Audiences are already accustomed to AI-derived insight on broadcasts, with AWS tools powering live strategy predictions, so the appetite for explanation exists.

The next advantage may not sit under the engine cover, but it will not replace what is there either. Mercedes won both 2026 titles with a car that was quick from the first race, and no strategy model would have changed that. In a sport where a title can turn on two points, however, a team that decides faster and more accurately with the same machinery will collect points its rivals leave behind. The teams most likely to prosper are those that treat AI as part of how the organisation works, rather than as a partnership to be displayed, and that keep people accountable for the decisions it informs.
