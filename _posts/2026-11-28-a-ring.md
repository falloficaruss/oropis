Photonics is excellent at moving and mixing light and poor at remembering. A thermo-optic phase shifter holds its setting only while a heater keeps drawing power. A conventional nonvolatile phase-change cell keeps its setting without power; volatile behaviour can be engineered into it, but only by controlling its switching and relaxation dynamics. A neuromorphic building block that combines neuronal dynamics with memory needs more than either. It must accumulate input, let a volatile component decay over a characteristic timescale, and keep another component as a persistent state. Traditionally, these functions are assigned to different physical mechanisms.

This paper’s pitch is that a single flake of a layered crystal, copper chromium thiophosphate (CuCrP₂S₆, “CCPS”), laid on a silicon microring supplies all three behaviours through one state variable: the ring’s resonance wavelength. That is worth taking seriously, and worth pricing carefully. The data support a narrower claim than the abstract makes, and the narrower claim turns out to be the more interesting one. The question worth asking is not whether this device can imitate a neuron, but whether it has shown something more useful: a photonic element that retains, gradually forgets and consolidates information through a single physical mechanism.

## What was built, and what it did

The platform is a standard add-drop silicon ring, 45 µm in radius with a 460 nm wide waveguide, with Ti/Au electrodes on either side. An exfoliated CCPS flake roughly 55–60 nm thick was dry-transferred across the ring and electrodes through a window in the oxide cladding, after the passive circuit had already been fabricated; that is the “back-end integration” the authors stress. Mode simulations put about 5% of the guided power inside the flake, and because CCPS has a bandgap near 2.1 eV it is transparent at 1550 nm, so the overlap costs little loss.

Four results carry the paper.

Trains of +6 V, 250 ms pulses at 2 Hz push the resonance steadily to shorter wavelengths: about 210 pm after 1,000 pulses, an average of ≈0.22 pm per pulse, with the increments shrinking at higher counts. A short dose of 20 pulses gives a shift of ≈70 pm that decays to baseline in about a minute, with a fitted time constant of 27–29 s. A 1,000-pulse dose gives an initial shift of ≈270 pm, of which ≈130 pm remains, within error, throughout the 24 h measurement window. And with a laser parked 200 pm to the blue side of the unprogrammed resonance, normalised transmission at that wavelength falls from 0.72 to 0.10 over 1,000 pulses, crossing a chosen threshold of 0.2 somewhere between the 600- and 800-pulse measurements.

The authors read these as a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron: pulses are the inputs, the resonance shift is the membrane potential, the volatile relaxation is the leak, the residual shift is memory, and the fixed-wavelength readout is the threshold.

## From a copper ion to a shifted resonance

CCPS is a van der Waals stack of layers built from a sulfur framework holding chromium and P–P pairs, in which Cu⁺ ions sit in off-centre sites and can hop within a layer or across the gap between layers. An applied field tilts the energy landscape and moves the ions. Because the ions are a major part of the material’s dielectric response, redistributing them changes the local refractive index, and the ring converts that into something measurable: a resonance shift of Δλ ≈ λ·Δn_eff / n_g, where n_eff is the index the guided mode effectively feels and n_g is its group index.

Putting numbers on that (back-of-envelope, not from the paper): for a 460 × 220 nm silicon wire the group index is typically near 4.2, so a 270 pm shift implies Δn_eff of roughly 7 × 10⁻⁴. Divide by the 5% overlap and the average index change inside the flake is on the order of 10⁻², if you pretend it changed uniformly. That is large but not absurd for ionic redistribution. Compared like with like, the modal estimate of roughly 7 × 10⁻⁴ is about four times smaller than the effective-index tuning of roughly 2.8 × 10⁻³ that the same group reported in its 2024 Light: Science & Applications paper, on a different device and geometry. On the same assumptions the ring’s free spectral range (the spacing between successive resonances) is around 2 nm, so the full 270 pm excursion uses roughly an eighth of it.

Two features of the physics matter for everything that follows.

The first is the same-sign blue shift. Positive and negative bias both move the resonance to shorter wavelengths, a behaviour the group had already seen in 2024. The explanation offered is geometric: with lateral electrodes, either polarity drags Cu⁺ away from the centre of the waveguide toward one electrode or the other, so the ion population the mode samples falls either way. It is a neat explanation. It also means the device’s response, referenced to the initial state, is even in field polarity. Neither polarity is shown to undo it, and whether reverse-polarity programming can recover a programmed state is untested.

The second is the dose dependence. Cu⁺ has several accessible configurations, and the manuscript leans on prior work on the sister compound CuInP₂S₆ (Liang et al., 2025), which found three switching modes (within layers, between layers, and a coupled mode) selectable through pulse amplitude and width. Shallow rearrangements relax quickly; deeper ones persist. Repeated pulses push more ions into the deep configurations, which is the proposed reason 20 pulses leave nothing behind and 1,000 leave something that lasts at least a day.

Independent work from CIC nanoGUNE (Lancaster et al.) on vertical CCPS devices supports the general picture. They measure a Cu⁺ diffusion coefficient near 4 × 10⁻¹² cm²/s, show that switching rate sets the retention time, and find that relaxation follows a stretched exponential, exp[−(t/τ)^β], with β around 0.5–0.6; a β below 1 signals a broad distribution of relaxation times rather than a single one. They also suggest that “ferro” may be the least important syllable in “ferroionic”: they get robust ion-driven switching in CCPS with no measurable ferroelectricity, and note that ferroelectric, antiferroelectric and paraelectric behaviour have all been reported for it at room temperature. The ions are doing the work.

## Measured, inferred, and not yet shown

What was measured is clean and valuable: resonance positions against pulse count, resonance positions against time after a pulse train, and transmission at a fixed wavelength. Everything else sits on inference, and some of it is thinner than the abstract’s confidence implies.

**The mechanism.** The manuscript says the shifts are “consistent with” Cu⁺ redistribution, and that is the right phrase. The optical constants of CCPS were measured by ellipsometry at zero bias on a SiO₂/Si substrate; no index change under field was measured directly, so the in-flake Δn is inferred from the ring. The evidence for ion motion in this device is a hysteretic nanoampere I–V curve and a blue shift for both polarities. Heating is excluded by sign (heat red-shifts silicon) and by power (nanoampere currents at a few volts dissipate tens of nanowatts), and free carriers in the silicon cannot explain a 24 h plateau, since they would not last that long. But interfacial charge trapping is not excluded, and the manuscript shows no control ring with electrodes and no flake, no composition map before and after, and no Raman signature of the kind Lancaster et al. use to track Cu⁺ leaving the lattice.

**The time constants.** The headline τ_V ≈ 27–29 s rests on a relaxation curve with, after t = 0, a single point clearly above the noise floor (≈25 pm at 30 s, with error bars near ±10 pm) before the data sit at zero. A two-parameter exponential fitted to that is close to an exact solve rather than a test of exponential form. For the 1,000-pulse case the fitted ≈148 s is plotted on an axis running to about 1,200 minutes, which squeezes a two-and-a-half-minute process into a sliver; a reader cannot check it by eye. Given the stretched-exponential result above, the single-exponential τ is best read as a label for one dose on one device, a conclusion the authors support themselves when they note that τ changes with programmed state.

**Consistency across the figures.** The 1,000-pulse excursion is ≈210 pm in the cumulative-programming section and ≈270 pm in the relaxation section. That may simply reflect how long after the last pulse each spectrum was swept, since the volatile part decays in tens of seconds, but the text does not say. A larger puzzle: 20 pulses produce ≈70 pm in Fig. 3(c), about 3.5 pm per pulse, while the first 200 pulses in Fig. 2(b) produce only about 35 pm, under 0.2 pm per pulse. That is an order-of-magnitude difference in per-pulse efficiency between two datasets with the same stated amplitude and width (the repetition rate of the 20-pulse run is not given). The three main datasets also sit on resonances near 1500, 1504 and 1538 nm, and the manuscript does not say whether they come from the same ring. The authors do list “state reproducibility” among the things to improve, and that is the honest place to leave it: “history-dependent” currently includes whatever history that particular flake had before the experiment.

**The 24-hour plateau.** Silicon rings typically drift by several tens of picometres per kelvin. A plateau of ≈130 pm tracked for a day, with error bars near a tenth of its height, needs temperature held to a fraction of a kelvin, or a reference ring subtracted from the data. The manuscript mentions reference rings for polarisation calibration and measurements “under ambient conditions”, but not whether drift was corrected. Probably it was; the text should say how.

**The neuron.** The threshold is a readout wavelength and a transmission level (0.2) chosen by the experimenters on the flank of a Lorentzian, and “800 pulses” is merely the first sampled state below it, with a sampling step of 200 pulses. That is a thresholded readout, not a firing event. No spike is emitted, there is no reset, and no regenerative output is demonstrated, so the authors show a thresholded optical readout rather than a complete neuron able to drive a downstream network. The formal mapping is also looser than it looks. Equation (3) writes the relaxation term with a positive sign, which would make the state grow rather than decay without input and contradicts the decaying exponential of Equation (1); the input function f is never specified, so the equation is a template, not a fitted model. Compare the phase-change ring neuron of Feldmann et al. (2019), where crossing threshold physically flips the ring and produces an output pulse, and which was assembled into a network of four neurons and sixty synapses.

## Right device, wrong name?

Consider what the data actually look like. A brief burst leaves a trace that fades over about a minute. A long, repeated burst leaves a trace that partly fades and partly stays. That is less how neurons behave than how synapses behave: short-term plasticity that decays over seconds to minutes, converting into long-term change when the stimulus is repeated enough. The time constants point the same way. Biological membrane time constants are tens of milliseconds, the laser-neuron literature runs up to a billion times faster than biology (Nahmias et al., 2013), and the CCPS leak is a thousand times or more slower than a membrane. This is not a fast neuron. It is better described as a slow, synapse-like photonic element with a consolidation mechanism, and the paper’s Fig. 3 is its best evidence.

The case for that reading firms up when the device is set beside the alternatives.

**Phase change.** The paper’s own discussion cites the Oxford–Münster result that Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ can be made volatile or nonvolatile in one device, with retention tuned over six orders of magnitude by optical power in the waveguide (Youngblood et al., 2019). So “volatile and nonvolatile in one material” is not new. What is new is how it is reached: electrically, with nanoampere currents, no melt–quench cycle, and in a material that does not absorb appreciably at 1550 nm. The leak is also passive and free, rather than set by a probe beam.

**Ferroelectrics.** Barium titanate on silicon gives eight-level nonvolatile phase shifters with switching energies as low as 4.6 pJ (Geler-Kremer et al., 2022), and a 2026 programmable gate array switches in 80 ns. The CCPS paper reports no energy figure. If the pulsed current resembles the DC sweep in Fig. 1(d), roughly 8 nA at 6 V, one 250 ms pulse costs on the order of 10 nJ and the 1,000-pulse dose about 10 µJ (back-of-envelope, not from the paper). That is about three orders of magnitude above the BTO switching energy, and a 250 ms pulse is roughly three million times longer than 80 ns. Energy per programming pulse is not comparable to energy per spike, either: the polariton LIF neurons of Tyszka et al. report under a picojoule per spike, while this device has demonstrated no spike, only a programming sequence that crosses a readout threshold. These are different operations and the comparison is deliberately crude, but the gap in speed and energy is real: this is not a competitor on either.

**Endurance and reset.** The paper shows 1,000 pulses. A hafnia-based ferroelectric on a silicon ring has reported 4 × 10⁴ cycles, and vertical CCPS memristors have shown 2 × 10⁴ (Ma et al., 2023). An ionic device moves atoms on every pulse, and Lancaster et al. warn that overdriving CCPS reduces Cu⁺ to metallic copper, forms filaments and leaves sulfur signatures in the Raman spectrum, irreversibly. They also find, in vertical devices, that reverse polarity resets the state. In this lateral geometry no reset is shown, and reverse-polarity recovery of a programmed state was not tested, so the 130 pm that persists is, in this manuscript, a one-way ratchet. A foundry would also want to know what a transferred flake of mobile copper sitting next to a silicon waveguide means for its process flow, and Lancaster et al. note that CCPS growth needs high temperatures, so scale would depend on transfer.

So where might it fit? In places where slow, zero-hold-power, history-dependent state is a feature: on-chip weights that trim themselves over seconds to hours, reservoir computing on slow sensor signals, adaptive calibration of rings that drift. That is a real niche, and “slow neuromorphic photonics” is a more defensible banner than a LIF neuron whose leak lasts half a minute.

## What would change the verdict

The paper already shows the interesting part: a back-end-integrated 2D ionic conductor gives a silicon ring a controllable, two-timescale memory with a free leak. Moving from existence proof to platform needs six things.

1. A reset: any electrical or thermal protocol that returns the resonance to baseline, repeatably.
2. Statistics: the same protocol across many rings and several days, with the spread in per-pulse shift and τ reported.
3. Controls and chemistry: a flake-free ring with identical electrodes and pulses, plus a direct signature of Cu movement before and after.
4. Honest dynamics: log-time relaxation after both doses, fitted with a stretched exponential, and 24 h retention with temperature compensation.
5. Costs: energy per pulse and endurance out to at least 10⁴–10⁶ cycles.
6. Cascading: one device’s thresholded output driving another.

None of these is exotic, and the group is well placed to do all six. If even the first two come back clean, the “slow synapse” reading becomes hard to dismiss. Until then, the best description of the result is the modest one: a ring that learns slowly, forgets on a schedule that depends on how hard it was taught, and, so far, has no demonstrated way to unlearn.
