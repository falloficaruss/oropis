On November 5, 2026, Agility Robotics, Inc. began trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker symbol AGLT following the completion of its merger with the special purpose acquisition company (SPAC) Churchill Capital Corp XI [1, 2]. Founded in 2015 by Damion Shelton and Dr. Jonathan Hurst as a spin-off from Oregon State University, Agility markets itself as the first publicly listed pure-play humanoid robotics company in the United States with active commercial deployments [2]. For institutional and retail investors seeking direct exposure to embodied artificial intelligence without buying diversified technology conglomerates such as Tesla or Alphabet, the listing offered a rare standalone equity vehicle.

The market's initial pricing of that scarcity, however, quickly gave way to a more sober reassessment of the company's near-term financial fundamentals. After closing its final pre-debut session on November 4 at $11.26 per share, AGLT opened on November 5 at $11.38 and closed its first full day as a combined public company at $11.51, well above the $10.00 SPAC trust benchmark [3]. Over the next six trading sessions, the stock experienced sustained selling pressure, declining to close at $7.58 on November 12 and finishing the regular trading session on Friday, November 13 at $7.36 (before dipping to $7.14 in after-hours trading), even after JPMorgan initiated coverage on November 12 with a Neutral rating and an $11.00 price target [3, 4].

Evaluating what investors are buying in AGLT requires separating three distinct analytical questions that are frequently conflated in public commentary:

1. **Engineering capability:** Has Agility built a functional bipedal humanoid robot capable of operating safely outside a research laboratory?
2. **Commercial viability:** Can the company manufacture, deploy, and maintain those machines at scale with positive unit economics and sustainable working capital?
3. **Investment attractiveness:** Does an agreed pre-merger equity valuation of $2.5 billion adequately compensate public shareholders for the execution, financing, and dilution risks embedded in the business plan [1]?

A rigorous examination of Agility's Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) registration statements, contractual disclosures, and industry benchmarks suggests that while the company has achieved meaningful mechatronic and safety milestones, its current valuation prices in several years of near-flawless commercial execution that its historical income statement and conditional order book do not yet substantiate.

### 1. The Bull Case: Operational Pedigree, Cooperative Safety, and Manufacturing Readiness

Any balanced financial evaluation of Agility Robotics must begin by acknowledging its technical achievements. Unlike numerous robotics startups whose public demonstrations rely on teleoperation or tightly scripted choreography, Agility has spent more than a decade solving the problems of dynamic bipedal locomotion and industrial logistics integration. Under CEO Peggy Johnson, who joined the company in 2024 after holding senior executive roles at Microsoft and Magic Leap, Agility has focused on moving its fourth-generation robot, Digit 4, from experimental pilots into structured warehouse workflows [2].

According to the company's SEC filings and October 2026 commercial updates, Agility has accumulated more than 65,000 hours of cumulative operational runtime across commercial and pilot settings [1, 5]. Its robots have been deployed or committed across nine customer facilities, involving blue-chip logistics providers and industrial manufacturers such as GXO Logistics, Schaeffler, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada, Mercado Libre, and agricultural equipment maker AGCO [1, 5]. In late October 2026, Agility expanded its relationship with GXO Logistics to add deployments at two additional facilities and signed an agreement with AGCO for a manufacturing pilot scheduled for 2027 [5].

Beyond field runtime, Agility has addressed two structural bottlenecks that have historically prevented humanoid robots from scaling in enterprise environments: cooperative human-robot safety and manufacturing capacity. Traditional industrial robotic arms require physical safety cages and bolted floor installations, which are expensive and disruptive to retrofit into existing brownfield warehouses. On September 15, 2026, Agility unveiled its fifth-generation platform, Digit 5, engineered specifically for higher payload stability, improved end-effector dexterity, and cooperative operation alongside human workers [6]. On October 1, 2026, the company signed a memorandum of understanding with FORT Robotics to co-develop an integrated three-part functional safety architecture, incorporating an offboard safety bridge designed to allow Digit 5 to operate uncaged in shared workspaces [7].

Finally, Agility has invested ahead of demand in physical production infrastructure. Its "RoboFab" manufacturing facility in Salem, Oregon has been designed for an eventual annual production capacity of up to 10,000 robots, supplemented by an engineering and development hub opened in Fremont, California in July 2026 [1, 2]. For bullish investors, these assets represent a defensible head start in practical warehouse integration. Yet a head start in engineering does not automatically translate into commercial maturity, as the company's historical financial statements demonstrate.

### 2. Revenue Quality, Related-Party Concentration, and the GAAP-to-Cash Reconciliation

The core tension in the AGLT investment thesis becomes evident when comparing its $2.5 billion agreed pre-merger equity valuation against its historical financial performance [1]. Across full-year 2024, full-year 2025, and the first six months of 2026, Agility's recognized revenue has remained at a pilot scale while operating outflows have expanded.

Two aspects of these figures require rigorous analytical scrutiny: the composition of the top line and the accounting reconciliation of the bottom line.

First, examine revenue scale and customer independence. In 2025, Agility generated $1.782 million in total revenue [1]. Of that amount, $1.132 million, or approximately 63.5 percent, came from related parties, defined as entities that are also significant equity holders or strategic investors in Agility (a group that includes corporate backers such as Amazon, via the Amazon Industrial Innovation Fund, and German industrial supplier Schaeffler) [1]. In the first half of 2026, total recognized revenue declined by 72 percent year-over-year to $174,000, of which $121,000, or approximately 69.5 percent, was generated from related parties [1].

From a corporate finance perspective, heavy reliance on related-party revenue during the pre-IPO or de-SPAC phase warrants caution. Strategic corporate investors have dual incentives: they evaluate warehouse automation technologies for internal operational efficiency, but they also benefit from validating the commercial narrative of a portfolio company in which they hold significant equity. When related-party sales are excluded, Agility generated just $650,000 in independent, arm's-length commercial revenue across all of 2025, and only $53,000 in the first six months of 2026 [1]. While the first-half 2026 revenue contraction largely reflects a transitional pause as customers await the next-generation Digit 5 platform rather than an abandonment of the product, it underscores that Agility is still operating in an early customer-validation phase rather than a commercial growth phase.

Second, a credible evaluation of Agility's cost structure must distinguish between GAAP accounting losses and actual cash consumption. At first glance, Agility's first-half 2026 operating loss of approximately $362.7 million appears to signal a dramatic acceleration in cash burn compared to its full-year 2025 net loss of $138.086 million [1]. However, the SEC registration statement clarifies that first-half 2026 operating expenses included approximately $252.5 million of incremental non-cash stock-based compensation (SBC) [1]. It is standard practice for late-stage private companies completing a public transaction or hitting executive equity milestones to recognize substantial non-cash SBC charges upon the modification or vesting of restricted stock units and options.

To measure the actual liquidity drain on Agility's treasury, investors must look at the statement of cash flows. Cash used in operating activities was $99.839 million in full-year 2025 and $86.2 million in the first half of 2026 [1]. Reconciling the $362.7 million GAAP operating loss against the $86.2 million operating cash outflow removes the distortion of non-cash equity compensation, yet the underlying cash reality remains challenging. An operating cash outflow of $86.2 million over six months represents an annualized cash burn rate of approximately $172.4 million, up roughly 73 percent from the 2025 cash burn pace, before accounting for capital expenditures required to tool RoboFab for Digit 5 production.

### 3. Deconstructing the $300 Million Conditional RaaS Commitment and Warrant Dilution

Because trailing revenue of $1.782 million cannot support a multi-billion-dollar valuation on its own, the primary pillar of the bullish valuation case is Agility's disclosure of more than "$300 million of committed multi-year orders" for its Digit 5 platform [1, 8]. To evaluate how much weight equity investors should assign to this figure, it is necessary to examine both its legal structure and its underlying unit economics.

In contract law and financial analysis, commercial arrangements fall along a spectrum:

* **Non-binding expressions of interest:** Letters of intent or memoranda of understanding that carry no legal obligation to purchase.
* **Conditional contractual commitments:** Executed commercial agreements that obligate the buyer to take delivery only if the supplier satisfies specified technical, operational, and schedule conditions.
* **Unconditional purchase obligations:** Binding take-or-pay contracts where the customer must purchase the equipment on a fixed schedule regardless of further development milestones.

Agility's $300 million backlog belongs in the second category: a conditional contractual commitment. According to the company's SEC filings, the entire $300 million commitment comes from a single related-party customer and covers 1,000 Digit 5 robots to be deployed under a three-year Robots-as-a-Service (RaaS) contract [1, 8]. In its October 30, 2026 filing, Agility noted that this figure "represents potential multi-year value expected to be realized over time, subject to satisfaction of contractual milestones, product features, and specifications, and does not represent current-period revenue" [8]. Because Digit 5 was only unveiled in September 2026, with early-access test deliveries scheduled for the first half of 2027 and general commercial availability targeted for late 2027, the conversion of this commitment into recognized revenue depends entirely on Digit 5 meeting the customer's engineering and reliability gates over the next several years [1, 6].

Even assuming Agility satisfies every technical milestone on schedule, treating the $300 million commitment as equivalent to an upfront industrial equipment sale misstates its financial mechanics. Because the arrangement is structured as a three-year RaaS contract with an embedded equity incentive, investors must evaluate five distinct economic variables:

1. **Ratable Revenue Recognition:** Under a three-year RaaS contract for 1,000 units, $300 million in total contract value equates to $300,000 per robot over three years, or $100,000 in annual recurring revenue per deployed unit. Rather than booking $300,000 upfront upon factory shipment, Agility will recognize revenue ratably over the 36-month service period of each deployed cohort, stretching top-line realization across 2027 through 2031 and beyond.
2. **Hardware Ownership and Upfront Working Capital:** In a RaaS model, the robotics vendor typically retains title to the physical hardware on its balance sheet (or finances it through an equipment leasing facility) while bearing the upfront cash cost of the Bill of Materials (BOM), factory labor, and shipping. If Agility builds and deploys 800 to 1,000 Digit 5 units across 2027 and 2028 at an initial manufactured cost of $70,000 to $100,000 per unit, it must disburse tens of millions of dollars in cash to suppliers months or years before collecting the offsetting monthly RaaS fees.
3. **Maintenance, Field Servicing, and Component Replacement:** Unlike software subscriptions, which carry 75 to 85 percent gross margins with near-zero marginal delivery cost, RaaS contracts obligate the vendor to maintain uptime service-level agreements (SLAs). Actuators, harmonic drives, batteries, and tactile sensors experience continuous mechanical wear in industrial operation. Until long-term mean-time-between-failures (MTBF) data is established for Digit 5, field maintenance and spare-parts expenses represent a major variable in RaaS gross margins.
4. **Milestone and Termination Provisions:** Because the contract is contingent on product specifications and milestone satisfaction, any engineering delay in Digit 5's bimanual manipulation software or safety certification pushes cash inflows further to the right while fixed factory overhead continues to accrue.
5. **Customer Warrant Dilution:** Crucially, the SEC registration statement discloses that the agreement with this related-party customer includes warrants issued to the purchaser that vest proportionately as the 1,000 robots are deployed [1]. In corporate finance, customer warrants issued alongside commercial agreements are treated as a reduction of the transaction price (contra-revenue) under ASC 606 accounting rules and represent direct equity dilution to public shareholders. While the exact customer identity and strike mechanics should not be speculated beyond the filing disclosures, the presence of vesting customer warrants means that converting the $300 million RaaS backlog carries an embedded equity cost that reduces the net economic return accruing to common stockholders [1].

### 4. De-SPAC Mechanics, the 2024 SEC Regulatory Regime, and the Dilution Overhang

Agility's decision to enter the public markets via a merger with Churchill Capital Corp XI, sponsored by former Citigroup executive Michael Klein, has drawn comparisons to earlier Churchill de-SPAC transactions such as Lucid Group (2021) and Oklo (2025). However, analyzing a 2026 de-SPAC requires updating the regulatory context rather than relying on 2021-era assumptions.

During the 2020 to 2021 SPAC boom, companies merging with blank-check vehicles frequently relied on the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (PSLRA) safe harbor for forward-looking statements to market multi-year revenue projections that would have faced strict liability in a traditional Initial Public Offering (IPO). In January 2024, however, the SEC adopted comprehensive new rules governing SPACs and de-SPAC transactions (17 CFR Parts 210, 229, 230, 232, 239, 240, and 249) [9]. Those rules took effect in July 2024 and stripped de-SPAC transactions of the PSLRA safe harbor for forward-looking projections, required target companies to sign the registration statement as co-registrants (exposing them to Section 11 liability equivalent to a traditional IPO), and mandated enhanced disclosures regarding sponsor compensation, dilution, and fairness determinations [9].

Why, then, did Agility choose a SPAC merger with Churchill XI in June 2026 rather than a traditional underwritten IPO, given that the old projection loophole had been closed by the SEC two years earlier? The explanation lies in valuation certainty and structured co-investment. In a traditional IPO with $1.782 million in trailing revenue and $174,000 in first-half sales, institutional bookrunners would struggle to price a book at a $2.5 billion valuation through standard price discovery. By merging with Churchill XI, Agility was able to negotiate a bilaterally agreed $2.5 billion pre-money equity value directly with the SPAC sponsor and use that valuation to anchor a concurrent $201 million Private Investment in Public Equity (PIPE) priced at $10.00 per share, led by contract manufacturing giant Foxconn alongside strategic insiders [1, 2].

The reception of that $2.5 billion valuation by public institutional investors is reflected in the final voting and redemption tally disclosed on November 2, 2026 [1]. On one hand, more than 84 percent of the votes cast at the Churchill XI extraordinary general meeting approved the business combination [1]. On the other hand, holders of approximately 61.5 percent of Churchill XI's Class A public shares exercised their right to redeem their stock for cash, removing approximately $261.8 million from the trust account and leaving $164.1 million in remaining trust proceeds [1].

These two data points, an 84 percent approval vote and a 61.5 percent redemption rate, are entirely consistent under standard SPAC mechanics. Institutional SPAC investors (primarily arbitrage and event-driven funds) are permitted to vote in favor of a merger while simultaneously electing to redeem their common shares for their pro-rata share of the trust account (roughly $10.00 plus accrued interest). Voting "yes" allows the transaction to close, preserving the value of any detachable warrants or sponsor incentives the fund holds, while redeeming the common shares eliminates downside equity risk if the fund believes the post-merger company will trade below $10.00 once the trust floor is removed. Consequently, the 61.5 percent redemption rate should not be misconstrued as a universal belief that Agility's technology will fail; rather, it demonstrates that over three-fifths of the public trust capital declined to underwrite Agility's equity at a $2.5 billion pre-money valuation when offered a risk-free $10.00 cash alternative [1].

Combined with the $201 million PIPE and $100 million in prior equity financing, the transaction delivered $465.1 million in gross capital raised ($365.1 million in gross closing cash from the remaining trust and PIPE before transaction fees) [1, 2]. While this provides Agility with a meaningful liquidity runway to fund Digit 5 development through 2027, public shareholders must account for a multi-tiered equity overhang detailed in the SEC filings:

* **Unrestricted PIPE Shares and Selling Securityholders:** Unlike legacy company insiders who are bound by standard post-merger lock-up agreements, the 20.1 million shares issued in the $201 million PIPE at $10.00 per share are not subject to the same 180-day lock-up restrictions and are covered by resale registration obligations [1]. Alongside other registered selling securityholders identified in the prospectus, these shares create a near-term supply overhang whenever the stock trades near or above the $10.00 threshold.
* **Legacy Venture Lock-Ups and Early Release Triggers:** Existing Agility shareholders and venture backers rolled their equity into the combined company subject to a standard 180-day lock-up agreement expiring around May 2027, though the SEC filing specifies conditions under which certain lock-up restrictions can terminate early, including share-price performance triggers [1]. Many of these early investors hold low cost bases, having purchased Series A-1 and Series A-2 preferred shares in April 2022 at $1.27 and $2.47 per share, respectively, alongside later rounds through Series C-3 ($2.1 billion valuation in September 2025) [1]. Even at a market price of $7.36, early-stage venture funds hold substantial unrealized gains that may motivate secondary distribution once contractual restrictions lift.
* **Sponsor Shares and Customer/Public Warrants:** Add in the Churchill XI sponsor promote shares, public warrants, and the related-party customer warrants tied to the 1,000-unit Digit 5 deployment, and the fully diluted share count expands considerably under any upside scenario [1].

### 5. Engineering Economics: BOM Curves, RaaS Working Capital, and Locomotion Trade-Offs

To move from a venture-stage cash consumer to a self-sustaining industrial enterprise, Agility's management roadmap projects shipping 800 Digit 5 units in full-year 2027 (following early-access deliveries in the first half of 2027 and general availability in the second half), scaling to 7,000 annual units by 2030 and 25,000 annual units by 2035, while expanding its committed facility footprint from nine sites today to as many as 25 by the end of 2027 [1].

Achieving profitability across that volume ramp depends on compressing the hardware Bill of Materials (BOM). Industry and filing estimates place the BOM for Digit 4 near $125,000 per unit, excluding factory overhead and field support, while management's long-term engineering objective is to reduce the per-unit BOM toward $30,000 at mass manufacturing scale [1, 10].

The urgency of that cost reduction is dictated by warehouse labor economics. In North American logistics facilities, a fully burdened human warehouse associate earns roughly $22 to $28 per hour, or $45,000 to $58,000 per year for a single 2,000-hour shift. For a two-shift operation (4,000 hours per year), a warehouse operator spends roughly $90,000 to $116,000 annually on human labor per station. If Agility charges $100,000 per year under a RaaS contract (the implied annual rate of its $300 million, 1,000-unit, three-year commitment), a single Digit 5 robot must deliver net productivity roughly equivalent to two human shifts to offer a compelling return on investment for the customer. If Digit 5's manufactured cost remains near $100,000 to $125,000 during the initial 2027 production ramp at RoboFab, and Agility must also pay for field technicians, replacement actuators, and FORT safety hardware out of its $100,000 annual RaaS fee, initial unit deployments will generate negative to flat gross margins during the first year of service, requiring working capital to bridge the manufacturing outlay.

This cost equation connects directly to the engineering debate over bipedal versus wheeled locomotion. At the conclusion of Agility's September 15, 2026 Digit 5 unveiling video, the company displayed a concept rendering of a humanoid upper torso mounted on a wheeled base [6]. Addressing the rendering, Agility co-founder Dr. Jonathan Hurst explained that while Digit itself will remain a two-legged machine, "wheeled robots are absolutely in our exploratory roadmap for a family of robots that focus on being human-centric and multi-purpose" [6].

Rather than viewing bipedal locomotion as an absolute technological flaw or an absolute superiority, investors should evaluate it as a question of application-specific economics. As Dr. Hurst has detailed in Agility's technical briefs, legs provide distinct mechanical and kinematic advantages in specific environments:

* **Uneven terrain and vertical obstacles:** Bipedal robots can climb stairs, step over floor thresholds, dock plates, and debris, and navigate spaces built exclusively for human pedestrians [6].
* **Compact footprint and dynamic reach:** A bipedal robot can dynamically shift its center of mass and use whole-body leverage to lift heavy totes from floor level or reach high shelving within narrow aisles where a wide, counterweighted wheeled base might struggle to maneuver [6].

Conversely, in brownfield and greenfield logistics warehouses with flat, polished concrete floors engineered for pallet jacks and autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), a wheeled base eliminates the continuous battery consumption required for dynamic two-legged balance, reduces the number of lower-body actuators and harmonic drives in the Bill of Materials, and lowers the mechanical complexity of the drivetrain. The strategic question for Agility's long-term Total Addressable Market (TAM) is what percentage of warehouse and automotive assembly tasks genuinely require the kinematic advantages of bipedal legs versus what percentage can be addressed at a lower hardware cost by bimanual torsos mounted on omnidirectional wheeled bases. By expanding its exploratory roadmap to include wheeled form factors, Agility acknowledges that flat-floor unit economics will likely demand multiple mobility configurations [6].

### 6. Global Competitive Dynamics and the Nuances of the FCC Covered List

Agility's valuation must also be benchmarked against both international humanoid manufacturers and well-capitalized domestic rivals. A comparison of 2025 operational disclosures highlights a striking divergence in commercial volume between U.S. and Chinese robotics vendors, alongside critical differences in product mix and pricing.

Comparing Agility's $1.782 million in 2025 revenue directly against Unitree's $121 million or UBTECH's $114 million without adjusting for product mix would be analytically flawed [1, 11]. According to Counterpoint Research's February 2026 global humanoid market study, 26 percent of global humanoid sales revenue in 2025 was generated from entertainment and commercial performances, and 22 percent came from AI data production and academic research, compared to 17 percent for intelligent manufacturing and only 6 percent for warehousing and logistics [12]. Unitree achieved rapid shipment scale and a 37.7 percent consolidated gross margin largely by selling compact, lower-priced humanoids (such as the G1 and H1 series) upfront to university laboratories, AI developers, and entertainment venues, which do not require the heavy payload endurance, multi-year RaaS uptime guarantees, or FORT functional safety architectures required on an American automotive or logistics floor [7, 11, 12].

Even with that product-mix qualification, the manufacturing volume achieved by Unitree, UBTECH, and AGIBOT drives rapid cost reductions across China's domestic supply chain for actuators, planetary roller screws, and force-torque sensors. In an unrestricted trade environment, Chinese vendors moving upmarket into heavy industrial warehousing would exert severe price pressure on U.S. robotics manufacturers.

Recent U.S. regulatory action has partially insulated Agility from that direct Chinese price competition, though the legal mechanism is more nuanced than a blanket statutory ban. On July 28, 2026, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) added foreign-produced "advanced robotic devices" (a category encompassing connected humanoid and quadruped robots) to the FCC Covered List on national security grounds, subject to exceptions for devices that receive conditional approval from relevant federal security agencies [13]. Subsequently, on September 18, 2026, the FCC published a public notice detailing procedures and potential exemptions for certain categories of devices [14].

Legally, placement on the FCC Covered List prohibits a device from receiving new FCC equipment authorization for its radiofrequency and wireless communication transmitters unless an exemption or conditional approval is granted [13, 14]. It does not automatically outlaw the physical possession or non-networked operation of every foreign robot in every setting, nor does it close the door on conditional approvals. In practical enterprise procurement, however, because industrial humanoid robots rely continuously on high-bandwidth Wi-Fi, 5G, and wireless safety bridges (such as Agility's offboard FORT safety link) to integrate with warehouse management systems, the denial of standard FCC equipment authorization creates a formidable compliance and cybersecurity barrier that discourages Fortune 500 logistics operators from procuring Chinese humanoids for U.S. facilities.

While the FCC Covered List restricts Chinese competitors in the U.S. warehouse market, it provides zero protection against domestic rivals. Agility must compete for enterprise contracts and engineering talent against Tesla (which is developing Optimus with tens of billions of dollars in balance-sheet liquidity and internal factory demand), Hyundai-backed Boston Dynamics (which is transitioning its all-electric Atlas platform toward industrial applications alongside its commercial Stretch warehouse robot), and heavily funded private U.S. contenders such as Figure AI and Apptronik.

### 7. Three-Scenario Valuation Model (2027 to 2030)

Relying solely on Agility's trailing price-to-sales multiple of roughly 1,400 times ($2.5 billion pre-money equity value divided by $1.782 million in 2025 sales) is insufficient for valuing an early-stage hardware company transitioning into commercial production [1]. A disciplined valuation must project Agility's revenue, unit economics, cash burn, and dilution across Bear, Base, and Bull scenarios through 2030, anchored by management's target of 800 Digit 5 units in 2027 and 7,000 annual units by 2030 [1].

| **2030E Active Deployed Fleet** | 1,800 units | 4,500 units | 10,000+ cumulative active units [1] |

**Bear Case (Probability: 35%):** Digit 5 encounters software reliability or dexterity delays during early-access testing in the first half of 2027, slowing conversion of the conditional $300 million single-customer RaaS contract [1, 8]. Annual deployments reach only 250 units in 2027 and a cumulative active fleet of 1,800 units by 2030. Because production volume stays below RoboFab's 10,000-unit capacity, per-unit BOM remains elevated near $85,000, compressing RaaS gross margins to 12 percent after field servicing. Operating cash burn plus upfront RaaS hardware outlays exhaust the ~$365.1 million in gross closing cash by mid-2028, forcing a highly dilutive secondary equity offering following the May 2027 lock-up expiration [1, 10]. Discounted back at a 15 percent cost of capital, equity value compresses below $2.00 per share.

**Base Case (Probability: 45%):** Agility successfully transitions Digit 5 into commercial service in late 2027, deploying 550 units in 2027 and building an active RaaS fleet of 4,500 robots by 2030 across 20+ customer facilities. Foxconn manufacturing integration helps reduce per-unit BOM from $125,000 toward $50,000 by 2030, lifting gross margins to 35 percent and allowing Agility to reach positive EBITDA in 2030 on ~$425 million in annual revenue. However, financing the upfront BOM for 4,500 RaaS robots while funding ~$150 million in annual R&D and corporate overhead consumes approximately $390 million in cumulative cash between 2027 and 2029, requiring a $150 million to $200 million capital raise in 2028 and triggering partial dilution from customer warrants [1, 10]. Applying a 6.0x multiple to 2030E revenue ($2.55 billion EV) and discounting to late 2026 at 15 percent yields a present enterprise value of roughly $1.46 billion, or approximately $5.20 to $5.80 per share after accounting for incremental dilution.

**Bull Case (Probability: 20%):** Agility hits management's roadmap on schedule, deploying 800 Digit 5 units in 2027 and scaling annual shipments to 7,000 units by 2030 across a diversified base of more than 100 enterprise customers [1]. High utilization at RoboFab and Foxconn supply-chain leverage drive per-unit BOM down to $32,000, enabling 52 percent blended gross margins and 22 percent EBITDA margins on $850 million in 2030 revenue. Upfront working capital is bridged primarily through non-dilutive equipment financing backed by creditworthy customers, keeping cumulative cash consumption within the $365.1 million closing cash cushion [1]. Valued at 8.0x 2030E revenue ($6.80 billion EV) and discounted at 15 percent, the present equity value supports $12.50 to $14.00 per share, validating JPMorgan's $11.00 target and rewarding early public buyers [4].

Probability-weighting these three scenarios (35 percent Bear, 45 percent Base, 20 percent Bull) yields an expected present equity value of roughly $5.30 to $6.00 per share, noticeably below both the $10.00 SPAC merger price and the November 13 regular-session closing price of $7.36 [1, 3].

---

### 8. Conclusion

Agility Robotics has earned its reputation as a capable engineering organization. Logging more than 65,000 operational hours across nine customer sites, constructing a 10,000-unit-capacity manufacturing facility in Oregon, and partnering with FORT Robotics to solve uncaged cooperative safety place Agility ahead of most Western startups attempting to commercialize bipedal machines [1, 5, 7].

Yet a sound technology company can still be an unfavorably priced stock. At its $2.5 billion pre-merger equity valuation, investors in AGLT were asked to look past $1.782 million in 2025 revenue (63.5 percent from related parties), a 72 percent year-over-year revenue decline to $174,000 in the first half of 2026 (69.5 percent from related parties), and an $86.2 million six-month operating cash outflow (even after properly stripping out $252.5 million in non-cash stock-based compensation) [1]. They were also asked to treat a $300 million conditional three-year RaaS commitment from a single related-party customer, accompanied by dilutive customer warrants and attached to a Digit 5 robot that will not reach general availability until late 2027, as sufficient foundation for a multi-billion-dollar capitalization [1, 8].

The institutional market's response has been rational: 61.5 percent of Churchill XI's public shareholders elected to redeem their stock for cash rather than hold equity at the deal valuation, and secondary-market trading compressed the share price from $11.51 on November 5 to $7.36 by the close of regular trading on November 13 [1, 3]. Until Agility demonstrates that it can convert its conditional Digit 5 backlog into independent, third-party RaaS revenue while driving its Bill of Materials down rapidly enough to avoid a dilutive 2028 capital raise, AGLT remains an asymmetric bet where common shareholders bear the full cost of hardware commercialization risk.
