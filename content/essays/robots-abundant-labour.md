# The Automation Question: Robots in a Country with Abundant Labour

*India has millions of people looking for better work, a vast informal economy and wages that remain low by international standards. Yet the country is increasingly attracted to automation. The question is not whether India should adopt robots, but which problems robots should solve, who captures the gains and who pays when machines change the value of human work.*

Imagine two factories. The first is in Germany. Its manufacturer struggles to recruit people for repetitive assembly-line jobs. Wages are high, the workforce is ageing and an unfilled position can constrain production. Installing industrial robots is an obvious business decision: machines are not affected by labour shortages and perform specified tasks consistently.

The second factory is in India. Outside its gates, people are looking for work. An assembly-line job may be one of the few reliable sources of cash available to them. A worker willing to perform repetitive tasks can be hired for a fraction of the cost of a comparable worker in Germany.

Should this factory install the same robots? The answer cannot be imported along with the machinery. Automation economics change when labour is abundant, wages are low, employment is informal and millions of households depend on work that richer countries have already mechanised. A calculation that makes sense in Stuttgart may be foolish in a small industrial unit outside Pune; a machine that is an obvious improvement in a sewer may be destructive if it displaces waste pickers without giving them a place in the new system.

India should not resist robotics. It should resist the idea that replacing inexpensive people is, by itself, evidence of economic progress. The country needs higher productivity, safer workplaces and globally competitive industries. The right test is not how many workers a machine removes, but whether it solves a real bottleneck, spreads gains beyond its owner and gives affected people a fair path into the new economy.

## The illusion of cheap labour

India's large workforce is routinely presented as an economic advantage. But abundant labour is not the same thing as abundant productive employment. Millions of people are technically employed without stable incomes, predictable hours, written contracts or meaningful social protection.

A [NITI Aayog analysis of Periodic Labour Force Survey data for 2023–24](https://www.niti.gov.in/node/2028) estimates that nearly 550 million of India's 634 million employed people—about 87 per cent—were informally employed under the report's definition. This includes casual workers, self-employed people, helpers in household enterprises and regular wage workers without social-security benefits. Agriculture accounted for about 290 million of these workers.

The [World Bank's India Country Economic Memorandum](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099022725232041885/pdf/P175027138446b0c21862819662d283ed24.pdf) reports that enterprises with fewer than ten workers accounted for 73.2 per cent of total employment in 2023. Informal enterprises accounted for around 70 per cent of manufacturing employment, while labour productivity in firms with fewer than five workers was estimated at only one-eighth of productivity in firms with more than 100 workers.

That is the real starting point for India's automation debate. The country does not simply have too many workers; it has too few businesses capable of employing them productively, paying them well and investing in better processes. Installing robots in a limited number of large factories will not fix that weakness.

Nor should low wages be mistaken for the full cost of human labour. A wage is only the visible part of the bill. Workers and their families may bear the cost of injury, exposure to dust or chemicals, lost income during illness and the absence of social protection. An employer's accounts may record a cheap shift; society absorbs some of the damage. Labour can look inexpensive partly because costs are pushed onto workers.

This does not mean machinery is automatically cheaper. Robots require capital, electricity, software, integration, maintenance and skilled supervision. A business with low wages and uncertain demand may rationally decide that a machine costs too much. Neither a worker's wage nor a robot's purchase price tells the whole story. India should compare full costs, including safety and livelihoods.

## Tasks, not occupations

The automation debate is often framed as a contest between a human job and a machine. That is too crude. Robots replace tasks more readily than entire occupations.

A robot could take over heavy lifting on a factory line while a worker monitors the process and checks quality. Precision spraying could reduce a farm worker's exposure to pesticides. Automated sorting could protect waste workers from hazardous material while leaving people to manage collection, exceptions and quality control.

Some tasks should disappear. Repeated heavy lifting, toxic exposure and entry into dangerous confined spaces are not traditions India must preserve. But where a machine can remove the worst part of a job without removing the worker's livelihood, that should be the first design objective.

Businesses should begin by identifying the bottleneck: speed, inconsistent quality, unsafe conditions, underused machinery or a shortage of a particular skill. A robot may solve one problem and do little for the others. Buying technology before diagnosing the problem is a reliable way to acquire an expensive showpiece.

## Where factory robots fit

Manufacturing is one of the strongest cases for industrial robotics in India. The country cannot build a large manufacturing base by relying indefinitely on low wages and manual production. Manufacturers must compete on quality, consistency, delivery time and scale. Robots can help when processes are repetitive and volumes justify the investment.

India installed a record [9,100 industrial robots in 2024](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-demand-in-factories-doubles-over-10-years/), up seven per cent from the previous year, according to the International Federation of Robotics. The automotive industry accounted for about 45 per cent of installations. Welding, painting and component handling are structured, repetitive and quality-sensitive operations.

Electronics, metal fabrication, pharmaceuticals, food processing and packaging offer other applications. Robotic machine-tending can extend operating hours; machine vision can detect defects; robotic welding can reduce exposure to heat and fumes. Here, automation is a production tool, not a fashionable accessory.

But the economics change for a small supplier producing short runs, changing designs or working with unreliable power and uneven orders. A large automotive plant can justify a specialised robotic cell; a garment unit receiving varied orders may need flexibility more than speed. A robot that waits for materials or requires a foreign technician can be less productive than a skilled operator and a well-designed workstation.

For much of Indian manufacturing, the priority should be modest automation: machine-tending, lifting assistance, inspection, packaging and collaborative robots. These systems can be adopted in stages. The aim should be to help firms produce reliably, win orders and grow—not to make headcount reduction the principal performance measure.

## Make the robot available, not expensive

This is where India's missing middle matters. Most small manufacturers cannot buy custom automation, employ a robotics engineer and maintain equipment that may be used only intermittently. Telling them to automate is like telling a small farmer to buy a combine harvester: the technology may be useful, but ownership economics are absurd.

One model is **Robotics-as-a-Service**. Instead of purchasing a robot, a manufacturer pays an integrator a monthly fee, a per-shift charge or a fee linked to machine-hours or output. The provider owns, installs and maintains the equipment; the manufacturer pays for capability rather than carrying the capital cost and technical risk.

There are trade-offs. A subscription can become expensive, proprietary software can lock in customers, and small firms may struggle to negotiate service levels. Contracts need transparent pricing, uptime commitments, data portability and clear exit terms. Still, a service model could let a firm test automation on one production line before making a large investment.

A second model is a **shared robotics centre inside an industrial cluster**. India's [Micro and Small Enterprises Cluster Development Programme](https://dcmsme.gov.in/MSE-CDProg.htm) supports common facility centres for testing, training and complementary production processes. States and industry associations could build on that framework with shared robotic cells, machine-vision labs and demonstration lines. Firms could book time, pay per use and get help adapting a process before buying equipment.

A third model is financing for productive upgrades. Banks may struggle to assess a robot whose value depends on software and process redesign. A dedicated credit-guarantee window could reduce lender risk for viable small-firm projects with a credible productivity case, maintenance plan and worker-training component. This is a policy proposal, not a claim that a robotics-specific guarantee already exists.

India should spend less public money subsidising isolated prestige purchases and more building shared infrastructure, service networks and technical skills. These arrangements will not make every small business a candidate for robotics; they will make it easier to find out which ones are.

## Small farms need access, not ownership

Agriculture presents a different automation problem. More than 40 per cent of India's workforce remains engaged in agriculture, even though the sector contributes a much smaller share of national output. The average operational holding was 1.08 hectares in the 2015–16 Agriculture Census; provisional figures for 2021–22 indicate that about 88 per cent of holdings were small. The [Reserve Bank of India summarises these trends](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=22781).

An autonomous harvester may be technically impressive, but selling one to a farmer cultivating a hectare is rarely sensible. The machine may be unaffordable, unsuitable for local terrain or crops, and idle for much of the year. The obstacle is not farmer backwardness; it is the economics of individual ownership.

India's better route is shared access. The government's Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization supports Custom Hiring Centres, allowing farmers to rent equipment rather than purchase it. A [2026 government statement](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2297647&lang=1&reg=48) says eligible centres can receive financial assistance of 40 per cent of project cost, subject to programme limits. Spread the cost across many users and expensive machinery can be used productively more often.

The priority should be equipment that solves actual bottlenecks: precision spraying, mechanical transplanting, small-scale harvesting, weeding, crop monitoring and post-harvest grading. Grading may prove more useful than a robot that imitates every action of a farmer. The test is whether machinery improves net returns, reduces physical strain and works across small holdings. If it pays for itself only for a large landowner, it may deepen the gap between farms.

## The warehouse trap

Warehouses and distribution centres are attractive environments for automation. Goods move through predictable spaces, and many operations involve repetitive transport, picking, packing and palletising. Autonomous mobile robots can carry materials between stations; automated guided vehicles can move pallets; robotic arms can support packaging. Large facilities with high, steady throughput may find these investments worthwhile.

But automation can also intensify work. A worker who once walked through a warehouse may now stand at a station processing a constant stream of items delivered by machines. Software may measure every second, rank workers and raise targets whenever equipment makes the line faster. A system introduced to reduce physical effort can become a tool for tighter surveillance and labour control. Whether productivity gains become shorter shifts, better pay and safer work—or simply higher targets—is a management choice, not a property of the robot.

## Who owns the value in waste?

The most uncomfortable automation question may be found in the waste economy. India's recycling system depends heavily on informal workers who recover value from what households and businesses throw away. They collect, identify, sort and sell paper, metal, cardboard, plastic and other materials through networks of scrap dealers and aggregators.

The scale is substantial, although estimates need to be read carefully. A [2024 India case study by GRID-Arendal and the International Alliance of Waste Pickers](https://wastepickersinternational.org/document/organising-and-integrating-the-informal-recycling-sector-in-the-solid-waste-management-sector-india/) estimates that the wider informal recycling sector involves around four million people, including waste pickers, small scrap aggregators, dealers and intermediaries. It cites a separate estimate of about 2.2 million people engaged specifically in waste picking. It estimates that waste pickers in Indian cities collect 15–20 per cent of municipal solid waste. These are estimates, not a national headcount, but they describe an economic system too large to dismiss as marginal.

Now imagine a municipality investing in an AI-enabled material recovery facility. Conveyor belts move dry waste past cameras and sensors. Software identifies material types; robotic arms pick selected objects and place them in separate streams. Such systems can improve throughput and reduce the need for people to handle contaminated material. They are worth testing, especially for predictable waste streams and dangerous operations.

But waste is not a standardised factory component. It arrives in irregular shapes, mixed with food residue, broken objects and contaminated packaging. A robot that repeatedly picks identical parts may struggle to grip a crushed packet or tangled plastic film. A [study published in *Waste Management* in October 2026 and catalogued by NIST](https://www.nist.gov/publications/robotic-sorting-and-artificial-intelligence-material-recovery-facilities-mixed-methods) describes continuing challenges in deployment economics, AI performance, data availability, speed, reliability and gripping. It notes industry interest in using AI for detection and quality control without attaching a robotic arm to every sorting task.

AI vision may help measure material composition, identify contamination or flag valuable streams. Automation may suit heavy handling or high-risk fractions. Human workers may remain better at exceptions and mixed materials. The right facility may combine sensors, mechanical separation and human judgement rather than pursue a fully robotic line.

The deeper question is who owns the value recovered. When a centralised facility takes control of recyclable materials, municipalities and contractors may capture revenue that once supported waste pickers and small scrap businesses. The machine may improve facility efficiency while impoverishing the people who built the recovery network. That is not an inevitable consequence of technology; it is a consequence of who designs the system and who gets a say in it.

Pune's SWaCH cooperative offers an alternative. It has organised waste pickers as service providers and cooperative members rather than treating them as disposable intermediaries. A [2025 account in *The Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2025/may/22/waste-collection-green-work-pro-poor-partnership-pune-india) described more than 3,850 members collecting household waste, educating residents about segregation and diverting material from landfill. The cooperative shows that municipal partnership can turn informal work into a more secure and environmentally valuable service.

Pune has also shown how badly transitions can be handled. In 2025, the city's mechanised collection pilot in Viman Nagar prompted SWaCH workers to say that existing routes and incomes were being disrupted without a clear plan for their inclusion. [Local reporting by *The Times of India*](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/pune/talking-point-what-pmcs-new-trash-collection-plan-will-mean-for-pune/articleshow/124833596.cms) and [an earlier report on the pilot](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/pune/garbage-collection-overhaul-affects-livelihood-of-swach-workers/articleshow/123816146.cms) documented those concerns. This was a mechanised collection change, not robotic sorting; the distinction matters. But a technology upgrade can displace people before anyone has decided what happens to their income.

The [Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=2219676&lang=2&reg=48), effective from 1 April 2026, require segregation at source into wet, dry, sanitary and special-care streams. Better segregation should improve recycling before waste reaches a sorting facility. Municipalities should integrate cooperatives and existing workers into collection, material recovery and facility operations. They could train members to operate equipment, monitor quality and manage exceptions; workers should have a formal role and a share in the value created.

No one should romanticise waste picking. The work exposes people to physical, chemical and biological hazards, as the [George Institute for Global Health has documented](https://www.georgeinstitute.org/our-impact/policy-statements-and-recommendations/health-and-well-being-of-waste-workers-in-india). The goal is to remove the danger without stripping workers of their livelihoods and the economic value they help recover.

## Sanitation is not wage arbitrage

There is one area where the argument for automation should be close to non-negotiable: hazardous sanitation. Workers should not have to enter toxic drains, sewers or confined spaces to do work that machines can perform remotely or mechanically. Sewer-inspection robots, remote-controlled cleaning equipment, mechanical sludge removal and specialised tools for hazardous industrial cleaning deserve serious investment.

The economics should not be assessed only against a sanitation worker's daily wage. A human life, an occupational illness and the long-term consequences of dangerous work cannot be reduced to a labour-cost comparison. Here automation is not merely a productivity tool; it is a means of preventing avoidable injury and death.

Mechanisation must still come with a transition plan. Existing workers can be trained as equipment operators, maintenance assistants, safety supervisors and inspectors. The purpose is to end dangerous work, not to make the people who performed it economically invisible.

## Five rules for automating India

India needs an automation strategy, but it should not copy the priorities of countries with different labour markets and industrial structures.

**1. Automate danger before cheapness.** Prioritise machines that remove workers from toxic, physically destructive or life-threatening tasks. A cost-benefit analysis that omits health and safety is incomplete.

**2. Use robots to expand productive capacity.** Support automation where it improves quality, reduces defects, meets delivery schedules and helps firms win markets. Jobs eliminated are not a measure of technological success. A factory that doubles output, upskills workers and raises wages may create more public value than one that produces the same output with half the staff.

**3. Make access affordable for small firms and farmers.** Expand shared facilities, rental models, service contracts and local maintenance networks. Public support should help a small business use a robot without having to become a robotics company itself.

**4. Make worker transition part of the investment.** When public money supports a labour-changing project, the applicant should disclose likely employment effects and provide a credible plan for affected workers. Training must lead to actual roles—not merely a certificate. In waste management, cooperatives should be involved before procurement decisions are made.

**5. Build Indian capability around the machine.** Develop domestic capability in robotic arms, motors, actuators, sensors, machine vision, controllers, industrial software and repair. India also needs technicians outside major cities. A machine serviced locally and adapted to Indian conditions may be more valuable than a technically superior import that sits idle when a component fails.

These principles do not require businesses to preserve every job forever. Firms must be able to change, and some tasks will disappear. But the public has a legitimate interest in how that change happens, particularly when subsidies, public contracts or municipal infrastructure are involved. Productivity gains are not automatically shared; their distribution depends on ownership, competition, bargaining power, social protection and policy.

## The factory India should build

Return to the two factories. In Germany, the robot may be the answer to a labour shortage and an ageing workforce. In India, the same robot may be right if it raises quality, makes production viable or removes a worker from danger. It may be wrong if it merely displaces inexpensive labour in a business that has not solved more basic problems of demand, skills, power supply or management.

India's choice is not between robots and people. It is between ways of organising work around technology. One treats workers as a cost to be removed and assumes the market will manage the consequences. The other uses automation to build capable firms, safer jobs and a more productive economy—and makes room for the people whose work is changing.

A country with abundant labour does not need to prove its modernity by building machines that replace as many people as possible. It needs machines that help people produce more, work more safely, earn more and share in the value they create. The quiet robot is not the one that makes people disappear from the factory floor. It is the one that makes their work less dangerous, more skilled and more valuable.
