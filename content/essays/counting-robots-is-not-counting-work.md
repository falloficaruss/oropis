# Counting Robots Is Not Counting Work

The humanoid robotics industry has found a number it loves to talk about: shipments. Global humanoid shipments approached 25,000 in the first half of 2026, according to IDC, marking growth of more than 432% year on year. China's AgiBot led the market, with more than 8,600 units shipped. Tesla has confirmed that Optimus production has begun and is targeting sales outside the company in late 2027.

Those are striking milestones. They suggest that humanoid robotics is moving beyond a market of prototypes and laboratory experiments. Manufacturers are building at greater scale, suppliers are developing production capacity, and customers are increasingly willing to buy machines that until recently seemed closer to science fiction than commercial equipment.

But shipment figures answer only one question: how many robots have moved through the market? They do not tell us how many are working, what work they perform, how reliably they perform it, or whether they create enough value to justify their cost.

That distinction should be at the centre of every discussion about the industry's progress. **Counting robots is not the same as counting work.**

## A shipment is a beginning, not an outcome

A robot can be manufactured, shipped, purchased, installed, deployed and productive. These are separate milestones, not interchangeable descriptions of success.

A unit delivered to a university laboratory counts towards shipments. So does a machine bought by a theme park for visitor entertainment, a company testing a new manipulation model, or a factory evaluating whether a humanoid can handle a particular operation. Each purchase may be useful to the buyer and valuable to the manufacturer. None, by itself, proves that humanoids are becoming productive workers.

The composition of the market matters as much as its size. IDC's analysis indicates that roughly 70% of first-half 2026 shipments were directed towards display and validation activities, including research, education, performances and data collection. That is a substantial decline from the approximately 84% share reported for 2025, and the shift towards real-world applications is encouraging. Yet it also means that the majority of the market is still associated with activities other than ordinary operational work.

Even the remaining share needs careful interpretation. A robot operating at a customer site is not necessarily autonomous, continuously useful or economically worthwhile. Pilot projects and early deployments can take place in real factories without proving that a business can rely on the machines at scale.

The headline growth is real as a signal of manufacturing momentum. It is not a reliable proxy for labour productivity.

## The demonstration economy is still a large part of the market

Research robots, educational platforms and demonstration machines are not useless. They help improve hardware, collect training data, test new control methods and introduce potential customers to the technology. Entertainment deployments can also have a genuine business case: a humanoid that attracts visitors or improves a retail experience may create value without replacing a worker on a production line.

The problem begins when all these uses are bundled together and presented as evidence of industrial adoption.

A robot performing a rehearsed routine at an exhibition has demonstrated that it can perform that routine. A robot greeting visitors at a theme park has demonstrated a service application. A robot manipulating components in a production workflow has taken a step towards industrial automation. These achievements deserve different labels because they answer different questions.

The industry needs to be more precise about what it means by a commercial deployment. A paying customer is important, but a purchase is not proof of productivity. A machine can be commercially sold while remaining an experimental asset. A company can make money selling robots long before its customers make money using them.

That does not invalidate the business. It simply means that the industry's growth should not be mistaken for proof that its central promise has already arrived.

## AgiBot shows both the promise and the measurement problem

AgiBot's position in the shipment rankings is a remarkable manufacturing achievement. Its reported output demonstrates that humanoid production is no longer limited to a handful of expensive prototypes. The company is also pursuing deployments across manufacturing, retail, tourism and other service environments.

Some of these projects are more compelling than a conventional product demonstration. Deployments in retail and theme-park environments place robots in front of real customers and require them to operate within existing services. Industrial projects, such as the work reported at Longcheer Technology's tablet-manufacturing operations, are more directly relevant to the productivity question because they involve defined production tasks.

AgiBot has published strong performance figures for its factory work, including high task-success rates and substantial task counts. Those results are promising, but they are company-reported metrics. To understand their significance, customers and observers need more information about the test conditions, the number of interventions, the operating hours, downtime, maintenance and the duration over which performance was sustained.

A successful pilot is evidence of possibility. It is not yet evidence of repeatable economics across hundreds or thousands of sites.

The same standard should apply to every manufacturer, including those with more recognisable names and larger investor profiles. A high shipment total establishes commercial momentum; it does not settle the question of whether the robots are useful enough to keep.

## The factory floor is a better test than the showroom

Manufacturing is an especially useful proving ground because its requirements are measurable. Factories have defined tasks, schedules, workflows and output targets. A robot can be judged by whether it completes a task safely, consistently and at an acceptable cost.

Figure AI's work with BMW is an instructive example. Figure 02 operated at BMW's Spartanburg facility over a ten-month period, handling components in a structured body-shop workflow. BMW reported that the robot helped move and position sheet-metal parts used in vehicle production, accumulating significant operating hours and handling tens of thousands of components.

This is the kind of evidence that makes the humanoid proposition worth taking seriously. The robot was not simply showing that it could pick up an object. It was contributing to a real manufacturing process over an extended period.

But a successful industrial pilot should not be stretched into a claim that general-purpose humanoids are ready for mass deployment. The environment was selected, the task was defined, and the system was integrated into a particular workflow. That is how industrial automation should develop. It is also why the result should be understood as a meaningful step, rather than the end of the journey.

AgiBot's reported work at Longcheer raises similar questions. Production-line tasks and measurable throughput are much more relevant than staged demonstrations. Yet performance claims need independent scrutiny and longer operating histories before they can establish a dependable picture of fleet-wide productivity.

The next question is not whether a humanoid can complete a task once. It is whether a customer can depend on it every day, across shifts, without an engineer constantly standing by.

## Tesla's production milestone is not a productivity milestone

Tesla's Optimus programme illustrates the distinction particularly well. The company has confirmed production activity at Fremont and has discussed a path towards external sales in late 2027. Its manufacturing capabilities make Tesla a serious contender in the race to produce humanoid robots at scale.

But the company's own comments also point to the work that remains. Reliability, durability, electromechanical design and the AI required for open-ended tasks are not minor finishing touches. They are central to whether a robot can be trusted outside a controlled environment.

Tesla has discussed using a large internal fleet, including an Optimus Academy, to train robots and generate experience. That may be a sensible way to improve the system. Physical intelligence requires more than a convincing demonstration: machines must learn how objects behave, how to recover from mistakes and how to act safely when conditions differ from their training data.

However, robots built for internal training are not the same as robots earning their keep at customer sites. They contribute to development, not necessarily to commercial productivity. A production line capable of manufacturing thousands of units is a manufacturing achievement. A fleet of thousands of machines operating autonomously and reliably would be a different achievement altogether.

Tesla's progress should be judged on both fronts, not collapsed into one headline.

## Autonomy is the missing number

One of the most important details missing from many deployment announcements is the amount of human support required to make the robot appear autonomous.

A machine may perform the physical movement while a remote operator monitors its actions, corrects errors or takes control when something unexpected happens. Remote supervision can be a reasonable part of an early commercial model. It may allow companies to gather data, support customers and improve a system while keeping operations moving.

But supervision changes the economics. A fleet that requires one human operator for every robot is very different from a fleet in which one person can supervise dozens of machines. If skilled technicians must repeatedly intervene, the labour has not disappeared; some of it has merely moved out of sight.

Manufacturers should therefore report intervention rates, teleoperation hours and the number of robots supported by each operator. They should distinguish between fully autonomous task completion, supervised autonomy and direct remote operation.

The location of the human does not determine whether the work is autonomous. The amount of human intervention does.

Reliability deserves the same scrutiny. A 99% success rate may sound impressive, but it can be inadequate for a workflow involving thousands of repeated operations. Reported success rates mean little without the denominator, the test conditions, the definition of a successful task and a clear account of excluded failures. A short demonstration cannot establish long-term durability.

What matters is not simply whether a robot can complete a task, but how often it can do so, how much help it needs and how quickly it returns to work after a failure.

## The economics will decide which robots survive

Businesses do not buy robots because they look impressive. They buy them because they expect a return.

That return must be calculated against the alternatives. A humanoid competing for a material-handling task is not competing only against a human worker. It may be competing against an industrial arm, a conveyor, an autonomous mobile robot, a specialised machine or a redesigned workflow.

The humanoid form can be valuable because human environments are built around human bodies. Stairs, doors, handles, shelves and workstations may make a mobile, two-armed machine more flexible than a fixed automation system. But flexibility is not automatically an advantage in every setting.

Bipedal locomotion, complex hands, balance and a large number of moving parts add engineering and maintenance challenges. If a wheeled robot or a conventional robotic arm can complete the same task more cheaply and reliably, a humanoid may be the wrong tool.

The total cost of ownership extends far beyond the purchase price. Customers must account for integration, software, batteries, spare parts, maintenance, downtime, safety systems, training and supervision. A robot that is inexpensive to buy can still be costly to operate. A more expensive robot can be worthwhile if it delivers enough productive hours and reduces bottlenecks.

The relevant question is simple: what does it cost to complete a task with the robot, and is that better than the available alternatives?

Until manufacturers can answer that with credible customer data, claims about the economics of humanoid labour remain mostly projections.

## The humanoid form is a tool, not a destiny

The enthusiasm for human-shaped machines sometimes obscures a basic engineering principle: the best robot for a job is the one that does the job most effectively.

Humanoids may eventually be valuable precisely because they can move through spaces designed for people and manipulate tools and objects designed for human hands. That is a powerful proposition. It could reduce the need to rebuild workplaces around automation and allow one platform to handle a wider range of tasks.

But not every job needs a robot that walks on two legs. Warehouses may be better served by mobile robots. Repetitive assembly may be better suited to fixed robotic arms. Cleaning, inspection and transport may call for specialised machines with fewer moving parts.

The future is unlikely to belong exclusively to humanoids. It will probably involve different robotic forms working alongside one another, each selected for the environment and task it handles best.

The industry should resist treating human resemblance as a measure of technological superiority. A robot does not become more useful simply because it looks more like us.

## A better scoreboard for the industry

Shipment totals are easy to communicate and useful for understanding manufacturing momentum. They should remain part of the industry's reporting. But they need to sit alongside metrics that reveal what happens after delivery.

A more honest industry scoreboard would include:

- **Active customer deployments:** robots installed and operating at customer sites, separated from research fleets, demonstrations and machines awaiting deployment.
- **Autonomous working hours:** time spent completing assigned tasks without direct human control.
- **Task reliability:** independently measured success rates under ordinary operating conditions, with failures and interventions clearly defined.
- **Human supervision:** operator-to-robot ratios, teleoperation hours and the frequency of human intervention.
- **Operational economics:** cost per completed task, total cost of ownership, uptime and comparison with alternative methods.
- **Customer expansion:** repeat purchases, renewed contracts and larger deployments driven by demonstrated value.
- **Durability:** maintenance frequency, component replacement, downtime and useful operating life.

These measures would make the market harder to hype and more useful to customers. They would also reward the companies doing the difficult work of turning prototypes into dependable products.

A manufacturer shipping fewer robots that operate reliably in production may be making more meaningful progress than one shipping a much larger number of machines destined for research and demonstration. The latter may be building an important foundation for the future, but it should not be mistaken for the same kind of commercial achievement.

## The next milestone should be measured in work

The humanoid robotics industry is not failing. Its manufacturing progress is real, and the technical advances behind it deserve attention. AgiBot's shipment performance, Figure's industrial pilot and Tesla's production efforts all point to a market moving beyond isolated prototypes.

But a manufacturing revolution and a productivity revolution are not the same thing. The first is measured in units produced. The second is measured in useful work delivered, reliably and at a cost that makes sense.

The companies that ultimately lead this market will not necessarily be the ones that announce the largest production capacity or ship the most machines in a single quarter. They will be the ones whose customers keep buying because the robots have become dependable parts of their operations.

The industry should celebrate the arrival of thousands of new machines. It should also be honest about what those machines are doing. Research robots are research robots. Demonstration machines are demonstration machines. Commercial service platforms can create value without replacing industrial labour. Productive robots must prove that they can repeatedly deliver work that customers need.

For years, the question was whether humanoid robots could walk, run, dance and manipulate objects. Those demonstrations helped make the technology visible. They are no longer the most interesting test.

The question now is much harder: how many robots can work a shift, finish their tasks without constant intervention, remain reliable over time and justify their cost?

Until the industry can answer that question with transparent, independently verifiable evidence, shipment growth will remain an incomplete measure of progress.

**We should stop counting how many robots leave the factory and start counting how much useful work they do after they arrive.**
