## The claim

There is a tidy argument going around this week. Private funding rounds let AI labs run on narrative, it says, and public markets will finally force their unit economics into the light. The occasion is real. Anthropic filed its S-1 on October 26 after a confidential submission in June, started its roadshow on November 10, and set a range of $70 to $78 for 825 million Class A shares. Press reports, though not the filing, have it pricing on Wednesday, November 18 and trading on Nasdaq as ANT on Thursday. Meanwhile OpenAI, according to reporting from the Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg, booked about $10 billion of revenue in the September quarter and lost roughly $10.9 billion on an adjusted operating basis.

I think the argument is right that an IPO forces disclosure and wrong about what gets disclosed. A registration statement forces accounting: defined terms, enumerated obligations, named risks. Unit economics are an interpretation laid over that accounting, and in this industry the interpretation is the whole debate. The first AI-lab S-1 made the facts harder to dispute. It did not make the framing any easier to settle.

## What a funding round could never tell us

Consider what the private era ran on. Anthropic’s last private round, $65 billion at a $965 billion valuation, closed in late May. A price like that rests on a few dozen sophisticated investors’ beliefs about a growth curve, numbers shared under confidentiality, and the fear of missing the next round. Revenue arrived as “run-rate,” a figure the company defines and the press repeats. Nothing obliged the company to say how much compute it had promised to buy, how concentrated its customers were, or what it cost to serve a token. A funding round gives you the right to ask, not the right to a documented answer.

A precision first, because the argument depends on it. The [amended S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm) contains audited annual statements for 2023 through 2025. The 2026 figures that make the headlines, including the first half, the nine months and the third quarter, come from unaudited interim statements, as do the commitment totals. They sit inside a document that carries legal liability for misstatements, which is a real constraint, but no auditor has signed off on them the way one signs off on a year. That distinction matters throughout what follows.

On revenue, the audited record shows $41 million in 2023 and $4.6 billion in 2025. The unaudited interim statements show $33.2 billion for the first nine months of 2026, against $2.5 billion a year earlier. The second quarter, at about $11.6 billion, was the first of the six quarters shown in which the company posted an operating profit ($974 million), according to [The VC Corner’s reading of the original filing](https://www.thevccorner.com/p/anthropic-s1-ipo-filing), and the amended filing puts the third quarter at $17.0 billion. These numbers used to be rumors. Now they are reported under a regime that punishes inaccuracy. The filing is not shy about blemishes either: it estimates that 5 to 7 percent of nine-month revenue came from customers later found to have violated its usage policy or commercial terms, and says that revenue is not expected to recur, as [one reader of the filing](https://blog.beerandcode.com.br/en/news/anthropic-s-1-filing-revenue-rule-breakers) quotes it.

Then obligations, the category no private round would have surfaced. The filing puts aggregate committed spend at about $900.9 billion, substantially all of it compute and infrastructure. About $451.6 billion is expected to fall due before the end of 2030, about $70.7 billion in the twelve months after September 30, and nearly all the rest between 2031 and 2045. Against that, the company held $131.8 billion of cash and short-term investments at September 30, which the filing projects would rise to about $212.7 billion after the offering and a concurrent private placement.

The number needs careful handling, and it is easy to overstate. It mixes purchase commitments, short-term commitments, and about $451 billion of leases that have been signed but not yet commenced. About $186.2 billion of it, according to the filing’s [contractual obligations section](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026073040/anthropicpbc-fwpxeeaprospea.htm), relates to arrangements with exercisable termination rights or conditions that must be met before they begin, such as a counterparty securing financing, facilities or regulatory approvals. A further $150 billion to $175 billion of estimated payments under arrangements with no fixed dollar commitment sits outside the total altogether. So “fixed obligation” is too blunt. The risk is the size of the commitments, their timing, how conditional they are, and how they relate to demand that has not yet shown up.

Then the rest of the inventory. No single customer exceeded 10 percent of nine-month revenue, and the top five accounted for about 25 percent. An earlier Wall Street Journal report on the confidential draft had two customers supplying roughly a fifth of first-half revenue, which is a different period and a different cut rather than a contradiction. About 80 percent of revenue is consumption-based. The nine-month net loss was $104.0 billion, of which $94.3 billion was non-cash change in the fair value of convertible notes, derivatives and SAFEs, which the filing attributes primarily to the rise in the company’s valuation. The GAAP operating loss was $1.26 billion, a margin of negative 4 percent. Stock-based compensation was $3.3 billion for the nine months, with $14.2 billion of RSU expense reflected in the pro forma balance sheet for awards that settle at the IPO and another $24.1 billion of unrecognized RSU expense to be recognized over roughly two years. And seven co-founders will hold 50.1 percent of the vote through a single Class F share, while the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board.

None of this is a verdict on whether the company is worth a trillion dollars. It is a map of the terrain, drawn by someone who can be sued if the map is wrong, and more than any funding round gave anyone outside the cap table.

## The margin that isn’t one

Now the specific claim, that the IPO reveals unit economics. Here the filing shows disclosure and definition coming apart in public.

Anthropic does not headline a gross margin. Analysts derive one from a footnote on functional expenses, and there are three defensible versions of it.

The first, around 85 percent, is a serving margin: revenue less cost of revenue, divided by revenue. Cost of revenue was $2.38 billion against $16.29 billion of first-half revenue, and about $4.8 billion against $33.2 billion for the nine months, which implies roughly 85.5 percent, up from around 12 percent a year earlier. The filing says substantially all of cost of revenue is compute serving customers. It answers the question of what it costs to serve a paying customer’s request.

The second, around 65 percent, is what the company keeps after channel costs. Anthropic sells through the cloud providers’ marketplaces, which accounted for 44 percent of nine-month revenue and 47 percent of 2025 revenue. It contracts directly with those customers and pays each cloud provider a fee, and the cost of that sits in sales, marketing and partnerships, a line that grew from $0.3 billion to $5.5 billion year over year. One analyst who works through this estimates that net of the fees first-half revenue is closer to $13.6 billion and the margin closer to 65 percent. That is an analyst’s estimate, not a filed figure.

The third, around 45 percent, treats all compute as the cost of goods. Compute and infrastructure expense was $18.8 billion for the nine months, about 57 percent of revenue, which leaves about 43 percent. For the first half the same arithmetic gives about 45 percent. This version includes training compute, which the company books in research and development, and free-tier compute.

Which one is the unit economics? Each answers a different question. At a normal software company the three would sit close together and the argument would be pedantic. Here they are forty points apart, and the gap is the investment case. Whether training compute is a cost of goods or an investment in an asset that depreciates depends on how long a model stays valuable, and no accounting standard can tell you that.

The company also chose which unit to show you. The filing introduces “revenue per dollar of compute,” which analysts report rising from about $0.40 in early 2025 to $2.08 in the second quarter of 2026. It is an illuminating metric, defined by the company, and its best reading belongs to the quarter in which the company looked strongest. The same care applies to Run-Rate Revenue, which the filing defines as the trailing 28 days of consumption fees multiplied by 13, plus monthly subscription fees multiplied by 12. Reporting on the amended filing puts it at $81 billion on October 31, while recognized third-quarter revenue annualized is about $68 billion. In a business growing roughly 47 percent a quarter, a metric built on the latest 28 days will naturally run ahead of one built on the full quarter. Neither is wrong. The SEC will make you define your terms. It will not make you adopt the terms your most skeptical reader would have chosen.

## We have seen this movie

Disclosure forcing a fight over definitions is not new. When WeWork filed in 2019, its prospectus offered “community-adjusted EBITDA,” a measure that stripped out large categories of ordinary cost, and the market’s reaction to that definition did more damage than any audited loss. Uber’s prospectus the same year showed how much it spent to attract and keep drivers and riders, and the stock traded below its offer price on day one. In both cases the facts were on the page. What disciplined the companies was readers who refused to accept the company’s chosen lens.

Anthropic’s presentation is far tamer. Cost of revenue is a conventional line, the filing reconciles its non-GAAP measures to GAAP, and the analysts who rebuilt the margins did it from the company’s own footnotes, which is what a working disclosure regime looks like. The same lesson applies in softer form. The prospectus is the company’s best-curated telling, and the discipline arrives in the second, third and fourth reports, when each period has to be read against the last and every definition either survives or does not.

## The quarter that moved the story

Then the third quarter arrived, and the 85 percent story met the thing the filing itself warned about. By press accounts of the amended filing, sequential revenue growth slowed from roughly 144 percent to 47 percent. Compute and infrastructure rose from 48 percent of revenue in the second quarter to about 58 percent in the third, which the filing’s own nine-month and first-half totals bear out. An operating profit of $974 million became an operating loss of about $567 million, which analysts attribute in part to roughly $5 billion of short-term compute bought at a worse price and about $2 billion of non-cash donation-matching charges. One brokerage estimates that revenue per compute dollar slid from $2.08 to $1.72. That is its estimate, not a filed figure.

The direction is the lesson. The company contracts compute twelve to eighteen months ahead of delivery, so today’s costs reflect capacity ordered when revenue was a fraction of what it is now, and when demand outruns that capacity it has to buy more at spot-like prices. Margin depends on timing as much as on the price of a token.

This is why “unit economics” is slightly the wrong frame for the sector, and why the airline analogy is tempting. A software company has a marginal cost near zero and is judged on gross margin and customer acquisition. An AI lab has a real but small marginal cost sitting on top of obligations of several hundred billion dollars that are, for the most part, payable whether or not demand arrives. Like an airline, a shipping line or a telecom, it carries heavy fixed capacity, operating leverage that cuts both ways, and an outcome that turns on how full the capacity is.

The analogy breaks in three places. An empty seat is a perishable loss, while idle compute can be redirected, and the filing describes compute as a single fungible pool directed between training, customer serving and internal use, wherever the marginal unit is most productive. A plane does not get 40 percent cheaper to fly per passenger when the next model ships, but the filing says its latest model cut customer cost per task by about that much, and that cheaper tasks drive more usage. And an airline’s market grows with the economy, while an AI lab’s grows with capability, which may be the very thing that makes the commitments worthwhile or worthless.

That also means revenue measured against the $900 billion is a coverage ratio, not utilization, and I should not have treated the two as one. Real utilization would require different disclosure: capacity delivered versus contracted, the share of that capacity in productive use, revenue per unit of deployed capacity, the cost of serving incremental demand, and cash flow against the obligations. The filing gives pieces of some of these. It does not give the whole picture, and it cannot give the part that depends on demand that has not happened yet.

## The comparison nobody could make before

The IPO does something genuinely new when it comes to comparison.

OpenAI, by the reporting, had revenue of about $10 billion in the September quarter, up roughly 50 percent from $6.7 billion in the second, and an adjusted operating loss of $10.9 billion, up from $10.1 billion. Its adjusted operating margin improved from negative 150 percent to negative 109 percent, its annualized run rate is near $50 billion, and the adjusted loss excludes about $2.6 billion of stock-based compensation. Anthropic, by press accounts, reported about $17 billion of third-quarter revenue and an operating loss of $567 million that includes stock compensation.

Look at the epistemics rather than the numbers. OpenAI’s figures are leaked investor materials, adjusted, and attributed to people familiar with the matter. Anthropic’s are in a regulated filing with unaudited interim statements and non-GAAP measures reconciled to GAAP. Anthropic also leads with an adjusted number: nine-month adjusted operating margin of positive 12 percent against a GAAP margin of negative 4 percent. So the real difference is not that one company adjusts and the other does not. It is that one company’s adjustments must be reconciled in a document that carries liability and the other’s need not be. The companies are also not the same animal. OpenAI serves a very large, mostly free consumer base alongside its enterprise business, while Anthropic leans toward enterprise and API consumption, so part of any margin gap is a strategy gap.

Still, the template now exists. When OpenAI files, which press reports suggest could be as soon as early next year, every choice it makes will be read against the first S-1: whether it reports a GAAP cost of revenue, how it treats training compute, whether it offers a revenue-per-compute figure, how fully it tabulates commitments. Competitors disciplining one another’s disclosure may be the most important mechanism here, and it works whether the stock rises or falls.

## What the market does not discipline

Disclosure is not accountability, and the skeptics of this argument deserve a fair hearing.

Price discovery is a mood, not a verdict. Earlier reporting had Anthropic aiming at about $2 trillion. After a sell-off in AI stocks and the commitments disclosure, the range implies roughly $916 billion to $1.02 trillion on the filing’s 13.09 billion post-offering shares, and up to about $1.4 trillion fully diluted, by third-party arithmetic. Then Bloomberg reported the book was several times oversubscribed. Both are true: the market read the footnotes, repriced, and still could not get enough. A demand curve tells you the price at which people will hold a risk, not whether they understood it.

Then there is circularity. The offering is accompanied by a $26.0 billion private placement of non-voting stock to Google, NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC, at the IPO price. At the midpoint, Google and NVIDIA each take about $10 billion. Several of those companies are also paid out of the compute commitments, and the prospectus itself lists, among its risk factors, that reliance on suppliers that are also investors and customers creates dynamics that could give rise to conflicts of interest. Disclosure makes the web visible. It does not say how much of the sector’s revenue growth depends on the web holding.

Then control. Public investors are being offered economics, not much governance. The founders’ Class F share carries 50.1 percent of the vote, the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board, together they can elect every director, and the company will be a “controlled company” under Nasdaq rules. The prospectus warns that the structure may effectively eliminate Class A holders’ ability to influence important decisions, including a change in control, and that the duties of a public benefit corporation may conflict with shareholders’ financial interests. That is a defensible structure for a company whose stated mission includes safety. Buyers at a trillion-dollar valuation should still say plainly that they are buying with their eyes open, and not treat the structure as incidental.

Finally there is the clock. The filing warns that models have behaved, and may again behave, in unintended or misaligned ways. Safety research is a cost line, and quarterly reporting makes every cost line visible and comparable. The first real test of a public benefit corporation with a long-term trust is not a good quarter. It is a bad one, and the quarterly cadence means we will eventually see one.

## What the filing settles, and what it does not

The filing settles that the top line is real enough to be reported under legal liability, even if the 2026 interim numbers are not yet audited. It settles that this is closer to a capacity-heavy infrastructure business than to software, with a commitment base near $900 billion that is conditional in parts and uncertain in timing. It settles that gross margin is a definition, with at least three versions, 85, 65 and 45, whose gap is where the real argument sits. And it puts the lab-and-supplier web on the record.

It does not settle whether demand will absorb the commitments, whether models keep their value long enough to justify counting training as an investment, or whether the three margins converge. Those are forecasts, and no filing contains one that can be audited.

Three things are worth watching. First, whether Anthropic keeps its cost-of-revenue line and its revenue-per-compute metric stable across quarters, since a favorable metric that appears once is a talking point. Second, the ratio of compute expense to revenue in the fourth-quarter report, as short-term compute rolls off or does not. Third, the definitions OpenAI chooses when its turn comes.

## The next filers

The most interesting implication may not be about Anthropic at all. Disclosure now arrives from both sides of a contract. On November 12, Nscale, one of Anthropic’s GPU suppliers, filed an [S-1/A of its own](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002110365/000119312526458722/ck0002110365-20261112.htm). It describes agreements with Anthropic providing for payments of up to about $44.6 billion, subject to delivery and service-availability requirements, and states that it had not yet obtained binding commitments for any of the financings required to perform them. A lab’s commitment table is a promise to pay. A supplier’s filing says whether the capacity behind it exists yet.

If more suppliers, cloud providers and rivals list, the cross-checks multiply, and the numbers become harder for any single company to frame alone. That, more than any one prospectus, is how the public markets may eventually discipline the definitions. It will take quarters, not a roadshow.
